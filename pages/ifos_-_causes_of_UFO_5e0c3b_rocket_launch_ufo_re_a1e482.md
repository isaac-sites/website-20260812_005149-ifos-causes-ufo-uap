## Introduction

Rocket launches can produce some of the most dramatic objects ever reported as strange lights or apparent UFOs. A vehicle climbing through twilight may trail an enormous glowing plume; [stage separation]({{ 'stage-separation/' | relative_url }}) can generate fans, lobes and intersecting clouds; and an upper stage releasing exhaust or residual propellant can create an expanding spiral hundreds or even thousands of kilometres from the launch site. To someone who did not know a launch was under way, the display may bear little resemblance to the familiar image of a narrow rocket flame.

The key is geometry. High-altitude exhaust can remain in direct sunlight after the observer below is already in darkness, making a diffuse cloud shine against a dark sky. In the near-vacuum of the upper [atmosphere]({{ 'atmosphere/' | relative_url }}) and space, gases also spread far more widely than an aircraft contrail. Rotation, manoeuvres and separate engine burns can then turn that illuminated material into shapes resembling jellyfish, shells, rings or galaxies. NASA has documented examples in which a [Falcon 9]({{ 'falcon-9/' | relative_url }}) exhaust plume looked like a spiral despite being the normal product of a launch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apod.nasa.gov/apod/ap240312.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apod.nasa.gov">[Astronomy Picture of the Day]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apod.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy Picture of the Dayapod.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Astronomy Picture of the Dayapod.nasa.gov</span></span></span>

## Why twilight launches produce enormous glowing plumes

The spectacular appearance of a twilight launch depends on the difference between conditions at ground level and conditions high above it. Shortly after sunset, an observer may already be standing under a darkening sky while a rocket climbing hundreds of kilometres away is still exposed to direct sunlight. The same situation can occur before sunrise. Sunlight striking exhaust particles, vapour or ice at altitude makes the plume conspicuous against the darker background. NASA describes this as the twilight effect: a plume high enough to remain sunlit can glow even though the observer below is experiencing night.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apod.nasa.gov/apod/ap240312.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apod.nasa.gov">[Astronomy Picture of the Day]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apod.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy Picture of the Dayapod.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Astronomy Picture of the Dayapod.nasa.gov</span></span></span>

This explains why the visual result can be so disproportionate to the rocket itself. The luminous shape is not necessarily a solid object surrounding the vehicle. Much of what the witness sees is an expanding volume of exhaust and other material being illuminated from a favourable angle. At altitude the ambient air pressure is extremely low, so rocket exhaust expands much more freely than it does near the ground. The visible cloud may therefore widen rapidly and acquire soft, translucent edges unlike those of an aeroplane, meteor or conventional searchlight.

The timing matters as much as the rocket type. Many launches occurring in full daylight produce the same physical exhaust without an especially memorable sky display, because the bright daytime background reduces the contrast. Likewise, a launch deep in the night may have less sunlight available to illuminate the relevant part of the plume. The most striking cases therefore tend to occur within particular viewing windows around local sunrise or sunset, although later orbital manoeuvres can create illuminated clouds well after the initial ascent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apod.nasa.gov/apod/ap240312.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apod.nasa.gov">[Astronomy Picture of the Day]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apod.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy Picture of the Dayapod.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Astronomy Picture of the Dayapod.nasa.gov</span></span></span>

## Stage separation can make one launch look like several objects

A launch does not consist of a single continuous burn. Multi-stage rockets change configuration during flight, and those changes can create abrupt alterations in [brightness]({{ 'brightness/' | relative_url }}) and shape that are easy to interpret as separate aerial objects.

Falcon 9, for example, is a two-stage vehicle. Its first and second stages physically separate, after which the second-stage Merlin Vacuum engine ignites to continue carrying the payload towards orbit. Depending on the mission, that engine may later restart. Payload fairings are also discarded, and the first stage may perform its own manoeuvres as it heads towards a landing or disposal trajectory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nro.gov/Launches/launch-nrol-69/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nro.gov">[National Reconnaissance Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nro.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Reconnaissance Office NRO &gt; Launches &gt; launch-nrol-69</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Reconnaissance Office NRO &gt; Launches &gt; launch-nrol-69</span></span></span>

From a distant observer's perspective, these events can produce several confusing effects at once. A point of light may suddenly brighten when an engine starts. A bright plume may split into differently moving components after stage separation. One element may appear to reverse direction relative to another because of perspective, while an expanding cloud remains hanging behind them. None of those apparent motions gives a reliable intuitive sense of the actual distances involved.

NASA has published a predawn Falcon 9 image in which the stage-separation plume was brightly illuminated by the Sun while the launch occurred roughly 45 minutes before sunrise. The example illustrates an important identification clue: a large luminous feature can become especially conspicuous precisely when the vehicle reaches high altitude and changes stages, rather than when it first leaves the pad.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apod.nasa.gov/cgi-bin/apod/apod_search?tquery=International+Space+Station" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apod.nasa.gov">[Astronomy Picture of the Day]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apod.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nasa.gov.</span></span></span>

This is one reason witnesses may describe a launch as an object that suddenly “opened”, produced a halo or released smaller objects. The description may accurately capture what appeared in the sky while still giving a misleading impression of its scale and nature.

## Spirals come from moving or rotating upper stages

The most alien-looking launch-related shapes are often not produced during the first few minutes of ascent at all. They can appear later when an upper stage is manoeuvring, venting residual material or conducting a burn.

A dramatic example appeared over Alaska on 15 April 2023. Aurora observers saw a large pale-blue spiral expand across the sky for several minutes. The event followed a Falcon 9 launch from California roughly three hours earlier. The Associated Press reported that excess material released at high altitude froze and reflected sunlight, while researchers explained that rotation of the stage could distribute the material into a spiral rather like water leaving a rotating sprinkler.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/alaska-sky-spiral-aurora-northern-lights-90e767058f328bb95bab62c3f5bed1cc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AP News Rocket science: Alaska sky spiral caused by Space X fuel dump &#124; AP News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AP News Rocket science: Alaska sky spiral caused by Space X fuel dump &#124; AP News</span></span></span>

That several-hour delay is important. It shows why checking only for a rocket leaving a nearby spaceport at the exact moment of a sighting can give a false negative. An upper stage may already be in orbit and thousands of kilometres from its launch site when it produces the visible phenomenon. The Alaska case involved a polar-orbiting trajectory that carried the stage into the right position for the released material to be visible against the dark sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/alaska-sky-spiral-aurora-northern-lights-90e767058f328bb95bab62c3f5bed1cc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AP News Rocket science: Alaska sky spiral caused by Space X fuel dump &#124; AP News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AP News Rocket science: Alaska sky spiral caused by Space X fuel dump &#124; AP News</span></span></span>

NASA highlighted another striking spiral in March 2024. A Falcon 9 launched from Vandenberg in California produced a plume photographed from Akureyri, Iceland. NASA described the apparently galaxy-like object as ordinary rocket exhaust unusually backlit by the Sun and attributed the spiral form to the rocket reorienting as part of its payload-deployment activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apod.nasa.gov/apod/ap240312.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apod.nasa.gov">[Astronomy Picture of the Day]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apod.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy Picture of the Dayapod.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Astronomy Picture of the Dayapod.nasa.gov</span></span></span>

## One launch can create sightings across countries

Rocket-related reports can occur surprisingly far from the launch site because the visible source may be a high-altitude or orbital stage rather than the ascending rocket near the ground.

On 24 March 2025, the National Reconnaissance Office launched NROL-69 aboard a Falcon 9 from Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida. The official NRO record confirms the launch date, vehicle and site. Later that evening, observers across Britain, Ireland and continental Europe reported a large blue-white spiral. Meteorological and astronomy specialists associated the display with material from the Falcon 9 upper stage reflecting sunlight at altitude.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nro.gov/Launches/launch-nrol-69/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nro.gov">[nro.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nro.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Reconnaissance Office NRO &gt; Launches &gt; launch-nrol-69</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Reconnaissance Office NRO &gt; Launches &gt; launch-nrol-69</span></span></span>

The geography initially seems counter-intuitive: a launch from Florida produced an unusual sight in European skies. But once the payload has been delivered and an upper stage has travelled along its orbital path, the launch pad is no longer a useful guide to where subsequent exhaust or venting will become visible.

This creates a recurring trap in UFO identification. A witness may reasonably conclude that “there were no launches near me”, yet locality is the wrong test. For launch-related phenomena, investigators need to consider the trajectory and elapsed time after launch as well as the launch location.

The same principle is evident in the 2023 Alaska spiral and NASA's 2024 Iceland example. In each case the remarkable display was observed far from the place where Falcon 9 originally lifted off.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/alaska-sky-spiral-aurora-northern-lights-90e767058f328bb95bab62c3f5bed1cc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AP News Rocket science: Alaska sky spiral caused by Space X fuel dump &#124; AP News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AP News Rocket science: Alaska sky spiral caused by Space X fuel dump &#124; AP News</span></span></span>

## Why rocket clouds appear to grow, stop or change direction

Witness descriptions of launch plumes often contain apparently anomalous motion: an object expands without falling, seems to slow abruptly, develops a hole in its centre or changes direction. These impressions follow naturally from watching a three-dimensional cloud at an unknown [distance]({{ 'distance/' | relative_url }}).

**Rapid expansion** is particularly characteristic. Material released into extremely thin air can spread through an enormous volume. Because the observer usually has no distance reference, angular expansion may be interpreted as an object physically approaching or increasing in size.

**Sudden changes in shape** can correspond to changes in propulsion or attitude. A stage may stop one burn, coast and later restart its engine. Falcon 9's second stage is specifically designed to restart its engine when mission requirements call for multiple orbital manoeuvres.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nro.gov/Launches/launch-nrol-69/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nro.gov">[National Reconnaissance Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nro.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Reconnaissance Office NRO &gt; Launches &gt; launch-nrol-69</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Reconnaissance Office NRO &gt; Launches &gt; launch-nrol-69</span></span></span>

**Multiple luminous components** may correspond to separated stages, exhaust regions or released payloads rather than a formation of independent craft. The geometry can be especially confusing when the observer sees the event nearly along the direction of travel.

**Apparent stationary behaviour** can arise when a cloud continues expanding after the stage that produced it has moved on. The remaining illuminated material may seem suspended in one part of the sky even though individual gas particles are dispersing.

These effects are strongest precisely because the usual visual cues used to estimate size and distance are absent. Against a nearly featureless sky, a cloud tens or hundreds of kilometres away provides no obvious scale.

## Matching a strange sighting to a launch

The strongest identification does not come from noticing that a rocket launch occurred somewhere on the same day. It comes from matching the sighting to a plausible **event time window**, trajectory and sequence of launch operations.

A useful check starts with the witness's most precise time and location. Investigators can then compare those details against official launch records from launch providers, spaceports or mission organisations. For example, the NRO records NROL-69 as a Falcon 9 launch from Cape Canaveral on 24 March 2025, while SpaceX maintains mission information for its launch programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nro.gov/Launches/launch-nrol-69/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nro.gov">[National Reconnaissance Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nro.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Reconnaissance Office NRO &gt; Launches &gt; launch-nrol-69</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Reconnaissance Office NRO &gt; Launches &gt; launch-nrol-69</span></span></span>

The comparison should then ask several more specific questions:

* **Was the launch shortly before the sighting?** A twilight ascent plume may appear within minutes, but later upper-stage events can occur hours afterwards.
* **Did the trajectory pass through or near the observer's visible sky?** A distant launch site does not exclude an orbital-stage explanation.
* **Was the high-altitude stage in sunlight while the observer was in darkness?** This is a strong condition for conspicuous white or blue glowing clouds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/climate-environment/2023/04/19/spacex-launch-spiral-northern-lights/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington PostMysterious spiral over Alaska likely a result of SpaceX launch - The Washington Post...</span></span></span>
* **Did the reported sequence fit launch operations?** Brightening, separation, a fan-shaped plume, a later expanding cloud or a spiral may correspond to engine burns, staging, attitude changes or propellant release.
* **Did widely separated witnesses report the same phenomenon at the same time?** A huge upper-atmospheric or orbital plume can be visible across a much larger region than a low-altitude aircraft or drone.

</div>

Precise timing can be unusually powerful. In the April 2023 Alaska case, researchers could connect the spiral with a Falcon 9 launched from California about three hours earlier, while the geometry of the polar trajectory helped explain why Alaska was in the viewing zone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/alaska-sky-spiral-aurora-northern-lights-90e767058f328bb95bab62c3f5bed1cc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AP News Rocket science: Alaska sky spiral caused by Space X fuel dump &#124; AP News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AP News Rocket science: Alaska sky spiral caused by Space X fuel dump &#124; AP News</span></span></span> In the March 2025 European case, the official launch record establishes a Falcon 9 departure from Florida earlier on the same day, providing the fixed chronological anchor against which the later European observations could be assessed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nro.gov/Launches/launch-nrol-69/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nro.gov">[National Reconnaissance Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nro.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Reconnaissance Office NRO &gt; Launches &gt; launch-nrol-69</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Reconnaissance Office NRO &gt; Launches &gt; launch-nrol-69</span></span></span>

## A launch correlation is strong evidence, but not an automatic explanation

Rocket launches are such conspicuous potential IFOs that it can be tempting to attribute any strange light occurring near a launch time to the rocket. That shortcut is not sound.

A convincing match should explain more than the date. The vehicle's trajectory, timing, direction from the observer, altitude and expected operational events should all be compatible with the sighting. When imagery is available, the evolution of the plume provides additional evidence. A rocket explanation becomes much stronger when an expanding spiral or cloud appears exactly where and when a known upper stage should be visible than when the connection rests simply on a launch having occurred somewhere that evening.

There is also genuine uncertainty in identifying the precise mechanism inside a known launch event. In the Alaska case, space physicist Don Hampton told the *Washington Post* that the cloud was consistent with either rocket exhaust or a fuel release but that he could not distinguish those alternatives without confirmation from SpaceX. That is a useful model of evidential restraint: the broader identification as launch-related can be robust even when the exact operation producing the cloud is not publicly confirmed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/climate-environment/2023/04/19/spacex-launch-spiral-northern-lights/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington PostMysterious spiral over Alaska likely a result of SpaceX launch - The Washington Post...</span></span></span>

Scientific observing networks provide another route to confirmation. A study of a February 2021 Falcon 9 upper-stage event over the Mediterranean used ground-based video measurements and US space-object catalogue information to reconstruct the trajectory and identify the artificial source. The researchers noted that increasing numbers of artificial objects can contaminate observations originally intended to detect natural fireballs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01004" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

## What makes rocket launches such convincing UFO lookalikes

Rocket phenomena occupy a distinctive place among causes of UFO and UAP reports because the underlying object really is unusual aviation technology operating in an unfamiliar environment. The witness may correctly perceive extreme altitude, strange geometry and a rapidly evolving luminous structure while still misidentifying what generated it.

Unlike a distant planet or ordinary aircraft light, a rocket can genuinely produce enormous expanding clouds, sudden bursts of light, separated components and apparently structured spirals. Those features are not evidence that the witness imagined the event. They are consequences of propulsion, staging, orbital manoeuvres and sunlight interacting with exhaust at very high altitude.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apod.nasa.gov/apod/ap240312.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apod.nasa.gov">[nasa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apod.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astronomy Picture of the Dayapod.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Astronomy Picture of the Dayapod.nasa.gov</span></span></span>

The most diagnostic combination is therefore not simply “a strange light after a launch”. It is a tight match between the sighting time, launch or upper-stage trajectory, twilight illumination and the characteristic evolution of the plume. When those elements line up, a display that initially looks unlike any normal aircraft can become one of the more precisely reconstructable categories of identified aerial phenomenon.
