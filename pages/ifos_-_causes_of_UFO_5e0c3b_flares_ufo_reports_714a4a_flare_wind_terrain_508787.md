## Introduction

A parachute flare does not simply fall straight down. Once its canopy opens, it becomes a slow-moving object carried by the surrounding air, so a crosswind can make sideways motion much more conspicuous than descent. Military fire-control manuals explicitly account for this: wind drift can carry an illumination flare away from the point beneath its original trajectory, sometimes far enough that the firing direction has to be adjusted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.marines.mil/Portals/1/Publications/MCWP%203-16.6%20%20Supporting%20Arms%20Observer%2C%20Spotter%2C%20and%20Controller.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: marines.mil">[U.S. Marine Corps]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">marines.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Marine Corps MCWP 3-16.6 Supporting Arms Observer, Spotter, and Controller</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Marine CorpsMCWP 3-16.6 Supporting Arms Observer, Spotter, and ControllerMay 30, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 30, 2026</span></span></span>

Terrain then changes what an observer can see. A bright flare descending beyond a dark ridge can remain conspicuous until its line of sight intersects the mountain, at which point it disappears abruptly rather than seeming to reach the ground. Cloud, mist and haze can produce related effects by hiding the flare or progressively suppressing its light. These mechanisms are especially important in UFO or UAP reports because an observer who cannot see the landscape, parachute or intervening [atmosphere]({{ 'atmosphere/' | relative_url }}) may perceive only an isolated light that drifts, appears to hover, and then vanishes.

## Why sideways drift can dominate apparent motion

The crucial distinction is between the flare's movement through the air and the movement seen from the ground. After parachute deployment, an illumination flare descends relatively slowly while being advected by the surrounding wind. US military guidance does not treat that lateral motion as a minor curiosity. A Marine Corps manual warns that during illumination missions the firing direction may not pass over the intended target specifically because of **wind drift of the flare parachute**. An Army training aid puts the point more simply: wind shifts the direction of a parachute flare.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.marines.mil/Portals/1/Publications/MCWP%203-16.6%20%20Supporting%20Arms%20Observer%2C%20Spotter%2C%20and%20Controller.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: marines.mil">[U.S. Marine Corps]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">marines.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Marine Corps MCWP 3-16.6 Supporting Arms Observer, Spotter, and Controller</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Marine CorpsMCWP 3-16.6 Supporting Arms Observer, Spotter, and ControllerMay 30, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 30, 2026</span></span></span>

This can produce a counter-intuitive sight from kilometres away. The vertical component of the motion is viewed against a huge and usually unknown [distance]({{ 'distance/' | relative_url }}), while the lateral component may carry the light noticeably across the observer's [field of view]({{ 'field-of-view/' | relative_url }}). A descending flare can therefore look as though it is moving mainly sideways, or even maintaining roughly constant altitude.

Long-burning aircraft flares give wind considerable time to create this effect. Federal documentation describes the LUU-2 high-intensity illumination flare as suspended beneath a parachute for approximately five minutes while burning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2013-03-29/pdf/2013-07305.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AUTHENTICATEDMarch 26, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 26, 2026</span></span></span> A five-minute interval is ample time for a wind-borne light to shift laterally while its downward angular displacement remains surprisingly inconspicuous to a distant witness.

The underlying problem was recognised long before modern UAP discussions. A US Army military-pyrotechnics engineering handbook described wind drift as a severe problem for aircraft-deployed flares and noted that both parachute- and balloon-supported systems tend to drift with the wind.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/554002365/AMCP-706-188-Military-Pyrotechnics-Design-of-Ammunition-for-Pyrotechnics-Effects" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AMCP 706-188, Military Pyrotechnics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AMCP 706-188, Military Pyrotechnics - Design of Ammunition For Pyrotechnics Effects &#124; PDF &#124; FuzeMarch 1, 1974...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1, 1974</span></span></span> In other words, sideways movement is not an anomaly that counts against a flare identification. It is an expected property of the technology.

Terrain can make the airflow itself less straightforward. The US Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) warns that winds around mountains can differ substantially from those measured only a few miles away, with stronger flow through passes, severe turbulence near abrupt terrain and powerful downdraughts on lee slopes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/Air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap7_section_6.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Aviation Administration Section 6. Potential Flight Hazards</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Aviation Administration Section 6. Potential Flight Hazards</span></span></span> A parachute descending through such an environment need not follow a perfectly smooth diagonal path.

That matters when interpreting witness language such as “changed direction” or “moved sideways”. Those descriptions can be observationally accurate without implying powered manoeuvring. What matters is whether the reported movement exceeds what winds at the flare's actual altitude and position could plausibly produce. Surface wind at the observer's location is not, by itself, enough to answer that question.

## How terrain hides descending lights

Mountains can transform a gradual physical descent into an apparently instantaneous visual event. Imagine a flare tens of kilometres away, descending behind a ridge that is itself nearly invisible in the night sky. From the observer's position the light remains visible above the ridge line, perhaps apparently hanging in empty sky. The moment its line of sight drops below that ridge, the terrain blocks it completely.

The witness therefore does **not** see the sequence that intuition might expect from a falling light:

flare high in sky → flare visibly descending → flare approaches ground → flare reaches ground.

Instead, the experience can be:

bright light → slow drift or apparent hovering → sudden disappearance.

The missing piece is the unlit foreground terrain.

The later, roughly 10 p.m. lights associated with the 13 March 1997 Phoenix Lights episode provide a particularly useful historical example of this geometry. Contemporary and retrospective reporting linked those lights to illumination flares dropped over a military range southwest of Phoenix. Reporting in the *Phoenix New Times* described the flare drop as occurring about 30 miles southwest of the city and noted the explanation that the lights disappeared as they descended behind the Sierra Estrella range.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.phoenixnewtimes.com/arts-culture/the-phoenix-lights-are-no-mystery-6661825/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: phoenixnewtimes.com">[Phoenix New Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">phoenixnewtimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the phoenix lights are no mystery 6661825</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Phoenix New TimesThe 1997 &quot;Phoenix Lights&quot; are no mystery &#124; Phoenix New TimesMarch 14, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 14, 2014</span></span></span>

For the terrain question, the important point is narrower: the Phoenix example demonstrates how an unseen mountain horizon can determine when distant lights cease to be visible. That is more diagnostic than simply saying that flares “burn out”. If different lights vanish at different apparent heights or in a sequence that follows the silhouette of an intervening ridge, terrain masking becomes a testable geometric explanation.

The effect is particularly deceptive because mountains that dominate a daytime view may almost disappear visually after dark. The FAA's aviation guidance treats “mountain obscuration” as a distinct hazard precisely because cloud and poor visibility can conceal high terrain and make the relationship between an observer and the landscape misleading.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/Air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap7_section_6.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Aviation Administration Section 6. Potential Flight Hazards</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Aviation Administration Section 6. Potential Flight Hazards</span></span></span> For a ground observer watching one brilliant point rather than navigating an aircraft, the perceptual trap is different but the underlying fact is the same: invisible terrain is still physically present and can abruptly terminate the line of sight.

## Cloud and haze create different disappearance points

Terrain is not the only boundary a descending flare can cross. Atmospheric layers can make a light fade, blink out or become intermittently visible even while it continues burning.

Cloud provides the clearest analogue to a mountain. A flare descending into sufficiently opaque cloud can disappear over a short interval because droplets scatter and attenuate the light between the source and observer. Mountainous country makes this especially complicated because cloud bases and terrain heights vary simultaneously. The FAA notes that clouds in mountain regions may form at altitudes well above the reporting station while nearby summits are obscured.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/Air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap7_section_6.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Aviation Administration Section 6. Potential Flight Hazards</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Aviation Administration Section 6. Potential Flight Hazards</span></span></span> A witness may consequently have no obvious visual indication that a distant flare has entered cloud adjacent to a ridge.

Low cloud is also strongly tied to the landscape. The Met Office explains that air forced upwards over hills can cool to its dew point and condense, producing cloud on the terrain; when cloud lowers onto the ground, visibility can become extremely poor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/guides/mountain/video" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: weather.metoffice.gov.uk">[Met Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">weather.metoffice.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Met Office Mountain safety</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Met Office Mountain safety</span></span></span> Thus a flare descending towards mountainous ground can encounter a complicated boundary of ridges, cloud banks and obscured summits rather than a clean horizontal “cloud base”.

Haze works differently. The Met Office defines haze as very small dry particles suspended in the atmosphere, commonly associated with dust, smoke or pollution, and notes that it reduces visibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/blog/2025/whats-the-difference-between-mist-fog-and-haze" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metoffice.gov.uk">[Met Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metoffice.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Met Office Mist, fog, and haze: What&#x27;s the difference?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Met Office Mist, fog, and haze: What&#x27;s the difference?</span></span></span> Unlike a solid ridge, haze normally attenuates a distant light progressively rather than providing a perfectly sharp occulting edge. But at night the subjective result can still seem abrupt: as the source weakens with increasing optical path or enters a denser layer, it can fall below the observer's threshold of visibility over a short period.

That distinction is useful when assessing footage. A light disappearing at a repeatable angular boundary consistent with a known ridge favours terrain masking. A progressive dimming is more compatible with burnout, increasing atmospheric attenuation or cloud penetration. Irregular fading and reappearance may point towards broken cloud. None is individually conclusive, but each makes different predictions that can be checked against geography and weather.

## Why the same flare can look different from two places

Terrain and wind also explain why witnesses separated by only a modest distance can give apparently conflicting descriptions of the same illumination display. Each observer has a different horizon and line of sight.

From one location, a flare may remain visible for nearly its entire burn. From another, a ridge may hide its lower trajectory several minutes earlier. A third observer may view it through a mountain gap. Even the apparent direction of motion changes with perspective: wind-driven displacement that is mostly across the line of sight for one witness may be partly towards or away from another and therefore look much smaller.

Local visibility adds another layer. Mountain forecasts explicitly distinguish visibility in clear air from the presence of low cloud, and the Met Office notes that conditions can vary substantially across upland areas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/guides/mountain/forecast" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: weather.metoffice.gov.uk">[Met Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">weather.metoffice.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Met Office Mountain forecast guide</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Met Office Mountain forecast guide</span></span></span> It is therefore unsafe to assume that all observers tens of kilometres apart were looking through the same atmosphere.

This has an important consequence for historical UFO investigations. Differences such as “the lights moved” versus “they stayed still”, or “they went out” versus “they continued descending”, do not automatically establish different objects. Before treating those reports as contradictory, an investigation has to reconstruct where each witness stood, the azimuth and elevation of the light, the terrain profile along that sightline and the winds and cloud at the relevant altitude.

## The strongest test combines wind with the horizon

Terrain-and-wind explanations become persuasive when they predict observations rather than merely accommodate them afterwards. A good flare [reconstruction]({{ 'reconstruction/' | relative_url }}) should ask whether several independent pieces fit together.

**Direction of drift matters.** If the candidate flare moved eastwards, winds at its estimated altitude should make eastward transport plausible. Military manuals establish that wind drift is expected, but the actual historical wind field is needed to test a particular sighting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.marines.mil/Portals/1/Publications/MCWP%203-16.6%20%20Supporting%20Arms%20Observer%2C%20Spotter%2C%20and%20Controller.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: marines.mil">[U.S. Marine Corps]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">marines.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Marine Corps MCWP 3-16.6 Supporting Arms Observer, Spotter, and Controller</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Marine CorpsMCWP 3-16.6 Supporting Arms Observer, Spotter, and ControllerMay 30, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 30, 2026</span></span></span>

**The terrain profile matters.** Plotting the observer's exact line of sight against digital elevation data or a daylight photograph can reveal where a descending source should disappear. A sudden loss at that predicted boundary is much stronger evidence than a generic resemblance to flares.

**Different viewpoints should produce different cut-off points.** If a mountain is responsible, moving the observer changes the ridge profile. Videos from separated locations should therefore not necessarily show lights disappearing at identical apparent positions.

**Weather should be checked at altitude, not only at ground level.** Mountain winds can be stronger and more turbulent than nearby surface conditions suggest, while cloud can obscure terrain at elevations different from those implied by a valley weather report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/Air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap7_section_6.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Aviation Administration Section 6. Potential Flight Hazards</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Aviation Administration Section 6. Potential Flight Hazards</span></span></span>

These tests also guard against overusing “flares” as a catch-all explanation. Sideways movement alone does not prove that a UFO report was caused by a parachute flare, and a disappearing light is not automatically hidden by a mountain. The value of wind and terrain is that both leave reconstructable constraints.

For flare-like UAP reports, that reconstruction can reverse the first impression. A light that seemed to hover may have been descending slowly at great distance. A light that seemed to travel horizontally may have been a parachute carried by crosswind. A light that apparently switched off instantaneously may simply have crossed an unseen ridge or cloud boundary. What appears from one viewpoint to be unusual aerial behaviour can therefore be the combined product of an ordinary descending flare, moving air and a landscape that the observer cannot see.
