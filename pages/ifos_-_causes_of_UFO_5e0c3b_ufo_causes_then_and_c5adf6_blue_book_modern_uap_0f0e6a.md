## Introduction

[Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}) and today’s US government UAP data tell a similar basic story with a strikingly different cast of objects. In both eras, most reports that investigators can resolve turn out to involve ordinary or human-made things. But the pool of plausible explanations has expanded. Blue Book investigators repeatedly dealt with aircraft, balloons, [astronomical]({{ 'astronomical/' | relative_url }}) objects, satellites and miscellaneous visual effects. Modern investigators still identify balloons and aircraft, but now also encounter unmanned aerial systems (UAS), dense satellite constellations, rocket launches, sensor artefacts and even unusual but mundane objects such as birds and jet packs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://prologue.blogs.archives.gov/2018/04/16/ufos-natural-explanations/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: prologue.blogs.archives.gov">[Pieces of History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">prologue.blogs.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pieces of History UFOs: Natural Explanations – Pieces of History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pieces of HistoryUFOs: Natural Explanations – Pieces of HistoryApril 16, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 16, 2018</span></span></span>

The important change is therefore not simply that people report different-looking UFOs. The environment being observed has changed. Most dramatically, AARO — the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office — reported in 2026 that improved modelling had allowed analysts to resolve 238 reports as satellite flaring. That is an explanation category whose present scale could not have existed during most of Blue Book.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/FY25%20UAP%20Annual%20Report/AARO_FY2025_Consolidated_Annual_Report_on_UAP.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP...</span></span></span>

## What Project Blue Book counted as identifiable

Project Blue Book provides the clearest historical benchmark because of its scale. The US Air Force programme accumulated 12,618 sightings from 1947 to 1969, of which 701 remained officially classified as unidentified. In other words, roughly 94 per cent of the total case collection did not finish in Blue Book’s unidentified category. The Air Force said its investigations produced no evidence that the unidentified cases represented technology beyond contemporary scientific knowledge or extraterrestrial vehicles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying ObjectsAugust 15, 2016 — 25 Jun 2024 — From 1947 to 1969, a total of 12, 618 sig...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

The categories behind those totals matter more for this comparison than the famous number of unexplained cases. National Archives material describes Blue Book as sorting reports into **identified**, **insufficient data** and **unidentified**. Identifiable causes included aircraft, balloons, [satellites]({{ 'satellites/' | relative_url }}), searchlights and flares; astronomical objects such as meteors, comets and stars; atmospheric effects; and some hoaxes or psychologically influenced reports. An Archives account of the files says military and private aircraft, weather balloons and satellites accounted for much of the identified material, alongside birds, [reflections]({{ 'reflections/' | relative_url }}) and spotlights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://prologue.blogs.archives.gov/2018/04/16/ufos-natural-explanations/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: prologue.blogs.archives.gov">[Pieces of History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">prologue.blogs.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pieces of History UFOs: Natural Explanations – Pieces of History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pieces of HistoryUFOs: Natural Explanations – Pieces of HistoryApril 16, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 16, 2018</span></span></span>

A particularly useful snapshot comes from Project [Blue Book Special]({{ 'report-14/' | relative_url }}) Report No. 14, a statistical analysis of 3,201 early reports. Its classifications show how strongly the explanation pool was concentrated in a few familiar categories: 817 cases were astronomical, 642 aircraft and 450 balloons. Together, those three categories accounted for almost 60 per cent of the entire sample, before miscellaneous explanations were added. The study separately retained 298 cases as having insufficient information and 689 as unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/government-ufo-lies/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Government UFO Lies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultGovernment UFO Lies - The Black Vault Case FilesJanuary 17, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 17, 2024</span></span></span>

That distinction between **unknown** and **insufficient information** is important. Blue Book did not always use “unidentified” simply to mean that investigators lacked enough information to make a judgement. Special Report No. 14 treated [missing-data]({{ 'missing-data/' | relative_url }}) cases separately from cases whose reported characteristics could not be matched to a known object or phenomenon. The precise proportions varied over Blue Book’s lifetime as reports were investigated and classifications changed, which is one reason its early statistical sample should not be treated as interchangeable with the programme’s final 12,618-case tally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/government-ufo-lies/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Government UFO Lies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black VaultGovernment UFO Lies - The Black Vault Case FilesJanuary 17, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 17, 2024</span></span></span>

What the dataset captures especially well is the identification environment of its period. An investigator confronted with an unexplained light or moving object had a comparatively concentrated shortlist: aircraft, balloon, astronomical source, atmospheric effect or a smaller group of miscellaneous causes. Satellites entered that list after the beginning of the Space Age, but the orbital environment was nothing like today’s.

## What modern UAP reports add to the explanation mix

Modern UAP reporting retains several Blue Book staples while adding sources created by technological change. The Office of the Director of National Intelligence’s 2022 annual report illustrates the transition. Of 366 newly added reports considered in that assessment, 163 were initially characterised as balloons or balloon-like objects, 26 as UAS or UAS-like objects and six as clutter such as birds, weather phenomena or airborne debris. Another 171 remained uncharacterised and unattributed at that stage. The report explicitly cautioned that an initial characterisation was not necessarily a final resolution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.odni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/Unclassified-2022-Annual-Report-UAP.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: odni.gov">[ODNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">odni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Unclassified 2022 Annual Report UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNCLASSIFIEDJanuary 10, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 10, 2023</span></span></span>

By the FY2024 reporting cycle, AARO had received another 757 reports, including 485 involving events during the covered period and 272 older events reported later. It resolved 118 during the reporting period to prosaic objects including balloons, birds and UAS, while another 174 cases awaiting final closure were subsequently resolved as balloons, birds, UAS, satellites and aircraft. At the same time, many reports remained unresolved because the available evidence was inadequate for attribution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.defense.gov/2024/Nov/14/2003583603/-1/-1/0/FY24-CONSOLIDATED-ANNUAL-REPORT-ON-UAP-508.PDF?ftag=MSF0951a18" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FY24 CONSOLIDATED ANNUAL REPORT ON UAP 508</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of WarFiscal Year 2024 Consolidated Annual Report on Unidentified Anomalous PhenomenaNovember 14, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2024</span></span></span>

The FY2025 report, released in July 2026, makes the changing explanation pool particularly visible. AARO received 319 reports and resolved 114 of them, while also resolving 256 cases inherited from earlier reporting periods. The resolved cases included balloons, satellites, birds, aircraft and UAS, plus one commercial rocket launch and one manned jet-pack case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Congressional-Press-Products/%5D/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Congressional/Press Products</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Congressional/Press Products</span></span></span>

AARO’s broader public resolution statistics make the contrast even clearer. For closed cases in its database covering reports dated from 1996 to June 2026, the largest categories were balloons, at 50.7 per cent, and satellites, at 34.4 per cent. UAS accounted for 7.2 per cent, followed by birds and aircraft, with smaller numbers attributed to rockets or missiles, jet packs, fireworks, natural atmospheric effects, sensor artefacts, lasers and ordnance. These percentages describe **closed AARO cases**, not all submitted UAP reports, so they should not be read as the probability that any new sighting has a particular explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/UAP-Reporting-Trends/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UAP Trends</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UAP Trends</span></span></span>

That caveat is crucial for comparing the eras. Blue Book and AARO do not constitute one continuous experiment. They use different reporting systems, operate in different institutional settings and draw from differently selected populations of witnesses and sensors. AARO itself warns that its holdings have collection biases: places with dense US military sensors and operations produce more reports. In FY2025, civilian pilot reports routed through the Federal Aviation Administration also accounted for 21 per cent of incoming reporting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/FY25%20UAP%20Annual%20Report/AARO_FY2025_Consolidated_Annual_Report_on_UAP.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP...</span></span></span>

The datasets can therefore show that the **mix of identified objects has changed**, but they cannot cleanly measure how much more common one physical phenomenon has become relative to another across 70 years.

## Satellites show the change most clearly

Satellites provide perhaps the strongest evidence that the modern identification problem is genuinely different rather than merely renamed. Blue Book eventually had satellites among its conventional explanations, but modern low-Earth orbit contains vast constellations whose visibility and geometry can create unfamiliar patterns of moving lights.

AARO has specifically examined satellite flaring — sunlight reflecting from satellite surfaces towards an observer. Its technical paper notes that modern communications mega-constellations place thousands of satellites in low-Earth orbit. Depending on their geometry and surfaces, satellites can produce diffuse reflections or much brighter, short-lived specular reflections known as flares or glints. Closely deployed spacecraft can also appear as conspicuous satellite “trains”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/Information%20Papers/AARO_Satellite_Flaring_Paper.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Correlations of Starlink Satellite Flaring with UAP Observations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Correlations of Starlink Satellite Flaring with UAP Observations...</span></span></span>

This is not merely a hypothetical explanation. Researchers have reconstructed an August 2022 event in which five pilots aboard two commercial flights over the Pacific reported unusual objects and obtained photographs and video. By combining aircraft-position information with orbital data, the researchers found that a recently launched Starlink group could reproduce the observation geometry. Separate astronomical research has likewise shown that Starlink spacecraft can produce unusually bright flares and applied that mechanism to a UAP report made by commercial pilots.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.08155" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

AARO’s own statistics show how much analytical technique can alter a case pool. Its FY2025 report says a newly introduced capability using three-dimensional modelling and simulation enabled analysts to resolve **238 previously reported UAP as satellite flaring**. It also reported that high-altitude UAP reports had fallen sharply as a share of that year’s reporting, partly because many earlier civilian-pilot observations estimated at 45,000–60,000 feet were subsequently identified as satellite flares.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/FY25%20UAP%20Annual%20Report/AARO_FY2025_Consolidated_Annual_Report_on_UAP.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP...</span></span></span>

This is a particularly revealing lesson from the modern data. A cluster of apparently similar unexplained reports can grow because a new object has entered the environment — but it can also shrink suddenly when investigators acquire a better identification method. The changing case mix reflects technology in the sky **and** technology used to analyse what is seen.

## Drones create another category Blue Book barely had to consider

Small unmanned aircraft create a second major discontinuity. Blue Book’s investigators certainly dealt with aircraft, experimental aviation and other human-made objects, but the modern sky contains inexpensive, highly manoeuvrable remotely piloted systems available to governments, businesses and ordinary consumers.

The 2022 ODNI assessment was already separating UAS or UAS-like reports from balloons and other clutter. AARO now lists UAS as its own substantial resolution category rather than folding them into conventional aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.odni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/Unclassified-2022-Annual-Report-UAP.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: odni.gov">[ODNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">odni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Unclassified 2022 Annual Report UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNCLASSIFIEDJanuary 10, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 10, 2023</span></span></span>

The distinction matters because a small drone can behave differently from the aircraft familiar to a 1950s observer. It may hover, change direction at low speed, operate at night and display bright LEDs while presenting little visible structure at distance. In restricted airspace, moreover, identifying something as a drone does not necessarily end the security question: investigators may still need to establish who operated it and why.

AARO’s FY2025 report illustrates this separation particularly clearly. It recorded 50 reports of UAS incidents near US nuclear infrastructure, weapons and launch sites, most involving hobby-sized or larger multirotor aircraft and with quadcopters the most commonly reported form. Crucially, AARO states that **none of those 50 incidents was reported as UAP**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/FY25%20UAP%20Annual%20Report/AARO_FY2025_Consolidated_Annual_Report_on_UAP.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP...</span></span></span>

That is an important modern refinement. “UAP” is not simply a synonym for “drone whose operator is unknown”. As recognition improves, investigators can move observations into a conventional object category even while other questions about origin, intent or airspace intrusion remain unanswered.

## Why unresolved does not mean the same thing across eras

The temptation in comparing Blue Book with modern UAP programmes is to put their unexplained percentages side by side and treat the result as a trend line. The datasets do not support that precision. Blue Book’s final 701 unidentified reports emerged from a programme operating under Air Force procedures of its own era. AARO works with different definitions, military and intelligence reporting channels, civilian aviation reports, modern imagery and sensor systems, and an “active archive” for reports that cannot currently be assessed because corroborating information is missing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying ObjectsAugust 15, 2016 — 25 Jun 2024 — From 1947 to 1969, a total of 12, 618 sig...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

In FY2025, for example, AARO moved 191 of the 319 incoming cases to that active archive because there was insufficient evidence to determine whether the underlying events represented ordinary phenomena or something beyond known technological performance. Only nine were identified as meriting further analysis by intelligence-community and science-and-technology specialists. AARO also stresses that a lack of timely, actionable sensor information remains a major constraint on resolution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/FY25%20UAP%20Annual%20Report/AARO_FY2025_Consolidated_Annual_Report_on_UAP.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP...</span></span></span>

NASA reached a closely related conclusion in its independent UAP study: the number of high-quality observations is too limited to support firm scientific conclusions, and future progress requires calibrated observations, multiple measurements and adequate sensor metadata.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/update-nasa-shares-uap-independent-study-report-names-director/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UPDATE: NASA Shares UAP Independent Study Report; Names Director</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UPDATE: NASA Shares UAP Independent Study Report; Names Director</span></span></span>

That means “unresolved” is primarily a statement about the relationship between a report and the available evidence. It is not, by itself, a description of the object’s nature. A case can remain open because it is genuinely puzzling, because a crucial measurement was never collected, because the witness’s estimate cannot be independently checked, or because analysts have not yet developed the technique or obtained the external dataset needed to identify it.

The satellite-flare cases make this concrete. Some observations that once occupied the unresolved pool became conventional after better orbital correlation and three-dimensional modelling were applied. The underlying event had not changed; the investigators’ ability to recognise it had.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/FY25%20UAP%20Annual%20Report/AARO_FY2025_Consolidated_Annual_Report_on_UAP.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP...</span></span></span>

## What the two datasets actually tell us

Read cautiously, Blue Book and modern AARO data support a stronger conclusion than a simple argument about whether UFOs are “real” or “explained”. They show that UFO and UAP reporting is partly an evolving classification problem.

Blue Book’s identifiable cases were heavily populated by the technologies and observing conditions of the mid-20th century: aircraft, balloons, astronomical objects and a smaller collection of satellites, atmospheric effects and miscellaneous causes. Modern reporting still contains several of those same categories — balloons remain especially prominent — but investigators now routinely have to test reports against UAS, huge satellite constellations, satellite glints, rocket activity and an expanding range of sensor effects and unusual human-made objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://prologue.blogs.archives.gov/2018/04/16/ufos-natural-explanations/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: prologue.blogs.archives.gov">[Pieces of History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">prologue.blogs.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pieces of History UFOs: Natural Explanations – Pieces of History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pieces of HistoryUFOs: Natural Explanations – Pieces of HistoryApril 16, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 16, 2018</span></span></span>

The continuity is as informative as the change. Balloons have not disappeared. Aircraft have not disappeared. Birds and astronomical misidentifications did not become obsolete when [drones]({{ 'drones/' | relative_url }}) and Starlink arrived. Instead, new technologies have been **added to the identification problem**, producing a larger and more complicated set of conventional candidates.

The most telling modern statistic may therefore be neither the number of reports nor the number left open. It is AARO’s ability to reclassify 238 reports through improved satellite-flare modelling. That result demonstrates how an apparently new class of UAP can emerge when the technological environment changes, and how part of that class can subsequently disappear from the unresolved pool once investigators learn how to recognise its signature.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/FY25%20UAP%20Annual%20Report/AARO_FY2025_Consolidated_Annual_Report_on_UAP.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP...</span></span></span>

Across the Blue Book and UAP eras, the useful question is consequently not just **“How many cases were unexplained?”** It is **“What objects existed to be mistaken for something unexplained, what evidence was collected, and what tools did investigators have to distinguish them?”** On those measures, the historical record shows substantial change: the modern sky contains more kinds of artificial objects, while modern investigators possess more powerful ways of identifying them.
