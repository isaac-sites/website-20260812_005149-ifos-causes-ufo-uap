## Introduction

Advertising aircraft can look remarkably unlike aircraft after dark. Some carry grids of computer-controlled lamps beneath the wings or on an external frame, producing scrolling words, symbols and animations while the aeroplane itself remains difficult to see. From the wrong angle or at sufficient [distance]({{ 'distance/' | relative_url }}), the readable message breaks down into changing rows, arcs or clusters of lights. A moving illuminated sign can therefore be reported as a rotating object, a luminous shape or a formation of lights with no visible airframe. The British UFO Research Association specifically identifies nocturnal advertising aircraft as a source of such reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bufora.org.uk/guide-to-ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bufora.org.uk">[BUFORA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bufora.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">BUFORAGUIDE TO UFOS &#124; BUFORA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BUFORAGUIDE TO UFOS &#124; BUFORA</span></span></span>

This is not merely hypothetical. In Washington, DC, in 1977, reports of a strange illuminated object were traced to a Cessna carrying hundreds of flashing advertising bulbs. Yet the reverse lesson is equally important: an advertising aircraft should not be declared the answer simply because one operates in the region. The strongest identifications match the sighting against the aircraft's actual time, route and operation.

## Unusual lighting configurations

Conventional aircraft lighting is designed primarily for visibility and collision avoidance, not to make an aircraft's shape obvious to somebody several miles away. At night, US aircraft normally show position lights and, where fitted, anti-collision lights; anti-collision systems may employ rotating beacons or strobes of differing intensities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/AIR_TRAFFIC/PUBLICATIONS/media/AIM_Chg_3_dtd_7-9-26.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Aviation Administration AIM Amd3 dated</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Aviation AdministrationAIM Amd3 dated July 9, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 2026</span></span></span> An advertising aircraft can superimpose a much brighter and stranger visual pattern on that familiar lighting.

Modern aerial night signs can be particularly deceptive because they need not resemble a conventional banner. One current US operator describes aircraft with roughly 300 computer-controlled lights mounted beneath the wings. The lamps can scroll text across the wingspan and display animated symbols, logos and patterns; among the animations the company explicitly advertises are representations of UFOs themselves. Its examples include words and messages apparently suspended against a dark sky with little or no readily visible aircraft structure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theskywriters.com/aerial-night-signs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theskywriters.com">[Skywriters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theskywriters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Skywriters The Skywriters Aerial Night Signs &#124; Glow-in-the-Dark Sky Messaging</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skywriters The Skywriters Aerial Night Signs &#124; Glow-in-the-Dark Sky Messaging</span></span></span>

The essential perceptual problem is that **the lights are what the observer sees, while the aeroplane becomes merely the platform carrying them**. Depending on viewing angle and the stage of a scrolling sequence, the same installation can produce several superficially different UFO descriptions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A line of lamps can look like separate objects flying in formation.
* Successive illuminated characters can resemble lights moving across a much larger object.
* Curves, logos or animation frames can create apparent discs, rings, crosses or geometric figures.
* Blank intervals in a programmed sequence can make lights seem to extinguish abruptly and then reappear.
* A slowly circling aircraft can make a luminous pattern appear to hover, reverse direction or repeatedly return to the same part of the sky.

</div>

These are not speculative capabilities inferred from UFO reports: changing text, animations and computer-controlled patterns are the advertised purpose of the equipment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theskywriters.com/aerial-night-signs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theskywriters.com">[Skywriters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theskywriters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Skywriters The Skywriters Aerial Night Signs &#124; Glow-in-the-Dark Sky Messaging</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skywriters The Skywriters Aerial Night Signs &#124; Glow-in-the-Dark Sky Messaging</span></span></span> BUFORA likewise describes US systems using matrices of lights either beneath aircraft or on trailing structures and notes that an oblique, distant view can turn the intended message into a confusing shifting pattern. It also points to [illuminated airships]({{ 'lighted-airships/' | relative_url }}) as a related source of reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bufora.org.uk/guide-to-ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bufora.org.uk">[BUFORA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bufora.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">BUFORAGUIDE TO UFOS &#124; BUFORA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BUFORAGUIDE TO UFOS &#124; BUFORA</span></span></span>

Aerial advertising has taken several forms over time, which matters when investigating older cases. Besides familiar daylight banners and skywriting, regulators have recognised skywriting, banner towing and the display of airborne signs as aerial-advertising activities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Notice/ND/N8900_20.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Aviation AdministrationNotice US DEPARTMENT OF TRANSPORTATIONSeptember 26, 2007 — 20 Sept 2007 — Aerial Advertising—Operating an...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 26, 2007</span></span></span> Lighted airships have an even longer history: electrically illuminated signs on blimps pre-date modern LED displays, so an apparently sophisticated animated object in an older report is not automatically an anachronism.

## Why the airframe disappears after dark

A witness may reasonably object that they would surely have recognised an aeroplane if one had been present. Night vision makes that less certain. The FAA notes that under low illumination the human visual system loses detail and colour discrimination and that identifying dim objects at distance becomes significantly harder. Bright illumination can also temporarily degrade dark adaptation and create glare.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap8_section_1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Aviation Administration Chapter 8</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Medical Facts for PilotsSince any degree of dark adaptation is lost within a few seconds of viewing a bright light, a pilot should close...</span></span></span>

That produces a strong contrast problem with advertising aircraft. The lamp array has been designed to attract attention from the ground; the fuselage, wings and external support hardware have not. Against a black sky those structures may reflect too little light to register clearly, especially when the observer is concentrating directly on the display. The result can be a set of sharp luminous points seemingly unsupported by anything physical. BUFORA's description of illuminated advertising aircraft emphasises precisely this effect: at distance and from an awkward angle, the intended billboard becomes an unfamiliar light pattern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bufora.org.uk/guide-to-ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bufora.org.uk">[BUFORA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bufora.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">BUFORAGUIDE TO UFOS &#124; BUFORA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BUFORAGUIDE TO UFOS &#124; BUFORA</span></span></span>

There is a second interpretive step. People naturally try to connect lights that maintain fixed relationships. Research discussed in the University of Colorado's UFO study showed how witnesses presented with multiple lights can progress from describing separate points to describing lights “in formation” and, in some reports, to perceiving an enclosing dark craft. The study called attention to an “airship effect” in which observers mentally infer a shape surrounding visible lights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s6chap02.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Sec VI, Chap 2 -- Perception, Conception, Reporting</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Files Condon Report, Sec VI, Chap 2 -- Perception, Conception, Reporting</span></span></span> This finding was developed from a different known stimulus, the Zond IV re-entry, but the perceptual principle is directly relevant to an illuminated aircraft: several lights moving together do not by themselves establish that the witness saw the boundaries of the object carrying them.

Distance further complicates matters because it removes scale. Without a visible fuselage, a small illuminated aeroplane relatively nearby and a much larger supposed object farther away can subtend similar angles in the sky. Apparent slowness is also ambiguous. An aircraft circling or flying partly towards or away from the observer can show little angular displacement, creating an impression of hovering even though it continues to fly normally.

## The Washington 'giant jellyfish' was an advertising Cessna

A particularly clear historical example occurred around Washington, DC, in June 1977. Residents of Prince George's County, including a Maryland state police officer, reported an illuminated object that was variously compared with a blimp or a large jellyfish. The reports generated calls to the police, Andrews Air Force Base and *The Washington Post*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1977/06/11/pr-georges-ufo-turns-out-to-be-airplane-flashing-an-advertisement/661b735d-4bdf-4956-8c22-6650cffe7bb4/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on washingtonpost.com.</span></span></span>

The source was subsequently identified by the aircraft's owner. According to the *Post*, the vehicle was a Cessna 150 fitted with **315 blinking bulbs beneath its wings**, used to display advertising messages. Its owner explained that the message was legible only within a relatively favourable viewing area; farther away, observers simply saw the luminous pattern. She also said the aircraft was flying the advertised route at approximately the time of the sightings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1977/06/11/pr-georges-ufo-turns-out-to-be-airplane-flashing-an-advertisement/661b735d-4bdf-4956-8c22-6650cffe7bb4/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on washingtonpost.com.</span></span></span>

That combination makes the case more useful than a superficial visual resemblance. Investigators had:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. an aircraft capable of generating the reported appearance;
2. an identified operator;
3. confirmation that it was actually flying that evening;
4. a location consistent with the reports; and
5. a lighting system whose unusual appearance independently explained why several people had failed to recognise an aeroplane.

</div>

The case demonstrates why advertising aircraft can produce sincere UFO reports. The witnesses did see an unusual airborne display; what was initially wrong was its interpretation, not necessarily the basic observation that something strange-looking was moving through the sky.

## A plausible explanation still has to match the flight

Advertising aircraft are also a useful warning against premature debunking. During investigation of the celebrated September 1965 Exeter, New Hampshire, UFO reports, a local illuminated advertising aircraft became an obvious candidate. However, investigator Raymond Fowler checked the operator's activity and reported that the Sky-Lite Aerial Advertising aircraft had not been airborne during the relevant period. Records associated with the subsequent congressional discussion likewise document the effort to compare the suggested advertising-plane explanation with its actual operations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-89hhrg50066O/pdf/CHRG-89hhrg50066O.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CHRG 89hhrg50066O</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CHRG 89hhrg50066O</span></span></span>

That does **not** establish what caused the Exeter reports. It establishes the narrower and methodologically important point that this particular proposed source could be tested and was not supported by its operating history. A hypothesis is stronger when it predicts details of the event; mere resemblance to a known UFO lookalike is insufficient.

The contrast with the 1977 Washington case is instructive. In Washington, the operator confirmed that the illuminated Cessna was in the relevant place at the relevant time. In Exeter, checking the candidate operator reportedly excluded its aircraft. Those are exactly the kinds of distinctions that should separate a defensible IFO identification from a convenient label.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1977/06/11/pr-georges-ufo-turns-out-to-be-airplane-flashing-an-advertisement/661b735d-4bdf-4956-8c22-6650cffe7bb4/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on washingtonpost.com.</span></span></span>

## Checking local flight operations and events

Because aerial advertising is deliberately deployed where people are gathered, the surrounding social context can be as useful as the visual description. Current operators market night signs for concerts, sporting events, nightlife, political campaigns, celebrations and other occasions, with flights specifically aimed at populated areas after sunset.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theskywriters.com/aerial-night-signs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theskywriters.com">[Skywriters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theskywriters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Skywriters The Skywriters Aerial Night Signs &#124; Glow-in-the-Dark Sky Messaging</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skywriters The Skywriters Aerial Night Signs &#124; Glow-in-the-Dark Sky Messaging</span></span></span> A cluster of reports near a stadium, beachfront, festival district or major public event should therefore prompt a targeted check for aerial advertising before more exotic explanations are considered.

A practical investigation works from the reported event backwards rather than searching for any convenient aircraft:

**Fix the observation first.** Record the closest possible time, viewing location, direction, duration and apparent path before consulting possible explanations. A vague report such as “sometime after nine” can make an incorrect aircraft appear to match.

**Look for event-related operations.** Check whether a sporting event, concert, political rally, festival or promotional campaign was operating nearby. Advertising companies often publicise unusual aerial campaigns, and event organisers, airports or local media may provide independent confirmation.

**Compare the light behaviour.** Scrolling, sequential flashing, periodic blank intervals and the repetition of the same pattern are especially useful clues. If witnesses occasionally glimpsed recognisable letters, numbers or a logo before the pattern became abstract again, an illuminated sign becomes a particularly strong candidate.

**Check actual aircraft activity.** Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast, or ADS-B, allows suitably equipped aircraft to broadcast position, altitude and movement information; the FAA describes ADS-B Out as transmitting such data approximately once per second. Commercial [tracking]({{ 'tracking/' | relative_url }}) networks can combine ADS-B with other surveillance sources and retain historical flight information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/about/office_org/headquarters_offices/avs/offices/afx/afs/afs400/afs410/ads-b" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Aviation Administration Automatic Dependent Surveillance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Aviation Administration Automatic Dependent Surveillance</span></span></span> A slow orbit or repeated passes over a crowd can be far more diagnostic than simply finding an aircraft somewhere in the wider region.

**Treat an absent public track cautiously.** Public [flight-tracking]({{ 'flight-tracking/' | relative_url }}) data are useful evidence, not a complete census of everything airborne. ADS-B coverage can depend on altitude, terrain and receiver geometry, and the FAA also operates programmes through which qualifying aircraft operators can limit how identifying information appears in public tracking feeds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/technology/adsb/faq" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on faa.gov.</span></span></span> Consequently, failure to find a track should lower confidence in an aircraft identification but does not automatically rule one out.

**Check regulatory records where appropriate.** In the United States, towing something behind a civil aircraft under the relevant rule requires operation under an FAA certificate of waiver, and FAA guidance specifically applies that system to banner-tow operators.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ecfr.gov">[eCFR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ecfr.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ecfr.gov.</span></span></span> Such records, together with airport and operator information, can provide independent corroboration for some aerial-advertising activity. A wing-mounted electronic sign is not necessarily governed by the banner-towing provision, however, so the regulatory trail depends on the particular installation and operation.

## What makes an advertising-aircraft identification convincing?

The best evidence is a convergence of appearance and operations. “It looked like an advertising plane” is weak; “an identified illuminated advertising aircraft flew this route at this time and its programmed display reproduces the reported sequence” is strong.

Several features raise the probability substantially when they occur together: a night-time sighting over or near a populated event; a slow, conventional flight path; regularly spaced lights; sequences that change in an organised rather than random fashion; repeated circuits; and independent evidence of a relevant aerial-advertising operation. Conversely, extraordinary apparent speed, a location far from the verified route or a time when the candidate aircraft was grounded can falsify the explanation.

This case family therefore illustrates a wider principle in UFO/UAP investigation without requiring a wider excursion into other causes. Illuminated advertising aircraft are unusual precisely because they are **engineered to attract the eye while disguising, incidentally, the familiar visual cues by which people recognise ordinary aeroplanes**. Their lights can be genuinely startling. But because they are commercial aviation operations, they also leave unusually testable traces: operators, routes, events, regulatory records and, increasingly, electronic flight data. Those records are what turn a plausible IFO explanation into an evidentially supported one.
