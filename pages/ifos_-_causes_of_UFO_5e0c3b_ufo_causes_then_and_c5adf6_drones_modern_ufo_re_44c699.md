## Introduction

A modern multirotor can hover almost motionless, accelerate or change direction abruptly, fly low enough to lack the familiar cues of conventional aircraft, and display lights that remain conspicuous after the body of the aircraft has disappeared into darkness. ## Why drones can look strange at night

The most UFO-like feature of a small drone is not necessarily its technology but the way that technology looks when ordinary visual references disappear. A multirotor does not have to keep moving forwards to remain airborne. It can hover, climb vertically, descend, stop and make relatively tight changes of direction. Those behaviours are entirely normal for the aircraft, yet they differ from the broad, predictable flight paths people associate with aeroplanes.

Darkness makes the difference more pronounced. Once a drone's frame and propellers are too dim or distant to see, an observer may be watching little more than one or several lights against an almost featureless sky. In the United States, small UAS operating at night under the main commercial operating rules are normally required to have anti-collision lighting visible for at least three statute miles, subject to permitted reductions when safety requires them. The rule therefore deliberately makes a small aircraft's light detectable at distances where its physical structure can be extremely difficult to discern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/CFR-2025-title14-vol2/CFR-2025-title14-vol2-sec107-29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">14 CFR § 107.29 - Operation at night. - Content Details - CFR-2025-title14-vol2-sec107-29January 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2025</span></span></span>

This creates a useful distinction between **what the drone is doing** and **what the witness can actually see**. The drone may simply be hovering 100 metres away, but if the observer sees only a bright point with no visible body, there may be no immediate indication that it is small or nearby. If it then moves sideways, climbs or turns, the motion can appear more unusual than it would in daylight.

The FAA's aviation-safety data show that encounters with unmanned aircraft are now routine enough to form their own continuing reporting stream. The agency says it receives more than 100 UAS-sighting reports near airports each month from pilots, citizens and law enforcement, and it publishes quarterly sighting records stretching back for years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/uas/resources/public_records/uas_sightings_report" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Aviation AdministrationDrone Sightings Near Airports &#124; Federal Aviation AdministrationJuly 1, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1, 2026</span></span></span> That does not mean every such report is definitely a drone; rather, it demonstrates how thoroughly small unmanned aircraft have entered the population of objects that people genuinely encounter in today's skies.

## How scale and distance become hard to judge

A light in a dark sky contains surprisingly little information about the object producing it. If its true dimensions and distance are unknown, apparent [angular size]({{ 'angular-size/' | relative_url }}) alone cannot tell an observer whether they are seeing a small nearby object or a much larger distant one. The same problem affects estimates of speed: apparent movement across the observer's [field of view]({{ 'field-of-view/' | relative_url }}) does not by itself establish the object's true velocity.

Research on visual line-of-sight operation of small drones illustrates how quickly the aircraft itself becomes a difficult visual target. A field study specifically examining the visibility and visual angle of a small drone found that maintaining line of sight depends strongly on viewing [distance]({{ 'distance/' | relative_url }}) and the tiny angular size the aircraft presents to the observer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/10/16/5501" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mdpi.com">[MDPI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mdpi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Distance and Visual Angle of Line-of-Sight of a Small Drone &#124; MDPIAugust 9, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 9, 2020</span></span></span> In practical terms, the lights can remain the dominant visible feature while details that would immediately identify the object as a quadcopter disappear.

That uncertainty can produce several familiar UFO-report characteristics:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **An apparently large object:** if the witness assumes the light is far away, its inferred size becomes correspondingly larger.
* **An apparently extraordinary speed:** a nearby small object crossing the field of view can seem extremely fast if it is mentally placed much farther away.
* **Abrupt manoeuvres:** multirotors can hover and change horizontal direction without following an aeroplane-like turn.
* **A seemingly silent object:** depending on range, wind and background noise, the observer may see the lights without clearly hearing the rotors.
* **A changing shape:** several lights on a moving or rotating drone can present different patterns as its orientation changes.

</div>

These are not proof that a particular UAP report involves a drone. They explain why distance information is so valuable in distinguishing an unusual-looking observation from genuinely unusual performance. The same principle is increasingly important in scientific UAP work: researchers developing instrumented sky surveys have found that apparently ambiguous trajectories can remain difficult to interpret without reliable distance, kinematic information or additional sensor modalities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.07956" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Commissioning An All-Sky Infrared Camera Array for Detection Of Airborne ObjectsNovember 12, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 12, 2024</span></span></span>

## A source pool that Blue Book did not have

The historical contrast is unusually clear. Project Blue Book investigated 12,618 UFO reports between 1947 and 1969, leaving 701 classified as unidentified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span> Its investigators worked in skies populated by balloons, conventional aircraft, experimental military aviation, [astronomical]({{ 'astronomical/' | relative_url }}) objects and other sources of misidentification. Small remotely piloted multirotors were not a mass-market aviation category.

Modern investigators face a radically different population. Commercial and recreational drone use expanded rapidly in the 2010s. The US Government Accountability Office reported that about 1.7 million small UAS were already being used for non-government purposes in the United States in 2019, including recreational and commercial aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/b-330570" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gao.gov.</span></span></span> Today, the FAA reports 837,513 registered drones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/exit/?id=gLIKZYBFoMeA4ZCoxTYxAS6EBoFJ%2B2DNJ2yvjzsXYSx3EZFEQKZ9eFvp1Bc7OZHOIIOCZx0MXSvtyl8kA6ywV%2F23YXund%2F5A0RVUQ5jLIrJcERf1%2FFendNjbyjn0IKHYKnfI%2FDf6WshW2njInyDG%2Bi%2FrWM%2BIZc8JkZ%2BrH3jZOsbPhlkNgyg%2Fd3U0g8CG3JNxAoXcc0byfyl0tQUrbPLJ2IgwASeS9sz6B8KzceaU90W1RTo06gc6qICl6a2zHS0%2BTMDTw9y5crTtlAdRcJ6YqUbvc905VVmPgoU%2Bv85N4AGjBVo0txDV9RQlxNaF5%2ByDMK5ZTaCvAmOXh6YJMJ1V0Dj71%2BbVJ4qtlHGbrsSzIUD8MZ2Fwh%2BMMC3lmnl2BGU0odijXh%2BW2bMXMcnwz%2Fe0DgS3gx2GF6HJyFaz6Y%2FoFTl05GmUrgkEja3PtCcvoG5F4ufdE2L6KwWANtFBFvDkjIbgtCmFa%2BTQya7A3oleRzrfXZroUb81CrXl0OMU1XhjddgT7zm%2ByRNEUbkgBZcuFAQ1iOtVxeh275cyLpxOew5SrYbOriKQmc90qjeH8SIA2FYdl1pAYqPCYvao1un68sL3MCDagUUBFTkiJCFbc0qw3Ygt7O1fa7%2BMhgyaKNY70piNvOsBP1p6I2ec2POHDv3Bs1t8k16qlsRTpFV%2F2BbUfRLF0aPNRr5Dh%2FnR8sRvCaZfYqRdymQuv9NWgWTsWg%2FHhRwuQa5ovgIZmu%2BywyPGBWA%2BIGb03I65L3yo1KffoNILfWM0X3lhgtLlMgZwRmb0at0dyF%2BPg4LRRdXY5cPWHC9F3TSFgMPgvzUcCHiMnYbIp1qZ4GR8gv%2Fzt%2BPsHnocI3rcTsbDF4AYgYNQu8dXjLkgxr4zQBKy2YRe8KJuCsu%2FO7b5%2BCi2tp08qtLQ2S8QxkfQCkjIKR4CgbakL72%2FerBCq4IKcLCg1fgTSCEosyoW4hPpBaoDTYkdxfei0nBjo1FCOgbXYOo%2FjA9TA5N2P3zbK%2ByJIRcrKkIEnoDzZsz1%2Fm06BAs1cAeZdKNx2A%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on faa.gov.</span></span></span>

Registration totals are not the same thing as the number simultaneously airborne, nor do they directly measure UFO reports. Their significance is demographic: an investigator examining an unexplained aerial observation today has a huge class of potential source objects that a Blue Book investigator effectively did not.

Small UAS are also unusually diverse. NASA technical work describes a category spanning fixed-wing aircraft, conventional rotary-wing designs, multirotors, ducted fans and other configurations, with small systems expected to operate extensively at low altitudes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20200002019" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ntrs.nasa.gov">[NASA Technical Reports Server]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ntrs.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nasa.gov.</span></span></span> That variety makes "drone" less like a single visual profile and more like a family of possible explanations. A witness cannot safely rule out UAS merely because an object does not resemble the familiar four-armed silhouette of a consumer quadcopter.

## Where drones appear in modern UAP classifications

AARO's statistics provide the clearest institutional evidence that UAS have become part of routine UAP triage. In its reporting data covering cases from 1996 through 15 June 2026, AARO lists **65 UAS resolutions among 908 closed cases**, representing 7.2 per cent. Balloons and [satellites]({{ 'satellites/' | relative_url }}) account for larger shares, but UAS form a distinct category alongside aircraft, birds, rockets, sensor artefacts and other ordinary sources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/UAP-Reporting-Trends/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UAP Trends</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO UAP Trends...</span></span></span>

The distinction matters because "UAP" describes the state of identification at the time of reporting, not necessarily the ultimate nature of an object. An observation can enter the system because a witness or sensor operator cannot identify it and later leave that unresolved category once sufficient evidence establishes that it was a drone. This is exactly why the modern source pool matters: adding a new class of real aerial objects inevitably creates additional opportunities for temporary non-identification.

AARO's broader workload shows the scale of that sorting problem. The Department of Defense reported that the office received 757 UAP reports in the reporting cycle covering May 2023 to June 2024 and was reviewing more than 1,600 cases overall.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/3964824/department-of-defense-releases-the-annual-report-on-unidentified-anomalous-phen/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on defense.gov.</span></span></span>(https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/3964824/department-of-defense-releases-the-annual-report-on-unidentified-anomalous-phen/) Its published reporting trends also show that the most commonly described UAP morphologies include "orb/round/sphere" and "lights" — descriptions that say relatively little about an object's physical identity when range and detailed imagery are unavailable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/UAP-Reporting-Trends/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UAP Trends</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO UAP Trends...</span></span></span>

Drone-related events also intersect with security reporting without necessarily remaining UAP cases. The 2024 UAP report recorded UAS activity around sensitive US infrastructure, illustrating an important analytical boundary: once an object is confidently identified as an unmanned aircraft, the remaining question may concern its operator, purpose or legality rather than its physical identity. An identified but unauthorised drone can be a serious security problem without being an unexplained phenomenon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/decade-2020s-aaro-fy2024-consolidated-report-2024-dod-aaro-consolidated-annual-report-on-uap-nov2024" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ufotransparency.com.</span></span></span>

## The New Jersey sightings showed the identification problem in real time

The wave of reported "drone" sightings over New Jersey and neighbouring states in late 2024 provides an unusually clear example of how complicated the modern aerial source pool has become. Federal agencies received numerous reports of apparent drones, but investigators warned that the reports did not all describe the same kind of object.

On 12 December 2024, the FBI and Department of Homeland Security said that they had not corroborated the reported visual sightings with electronic detection and that review of available imagery indicated many reports were actually lawful manned aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fbi.gov/news/press-releases/joint-dhs-fbi-statement-on-reports-of-drones-in-new-jersey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">joint dhs fbi statement on reports of drones in new jersey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">joint dhs fbi statement on reports of drones in new jersey</span></span></span> A subsequent joint statement from the DHS, FBI, FAA and Department of Defense said the sightings examined included a mixture of lawful commercial drones, hobbyist drones and law-enforcement drones, together with fixed-wing aircraft, helicopters and stars mistakenly reported as drones. The agencies said they had identified nothing anomalous in the investigated activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fbi.gov/news/press-releases/dhs-fbi-faa-and-dod-joint-statement-on-ongoing-response-to-reported-drone-sightings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fbi.gov.</span></span></span>

The episode is relevant to UFO identification precisely because the confusion worked in **both directions**. Real drones were present in the broader environment, making "drone" an entirely reasonable initial hypothesis. Yet some objects reported as drones were apparently conventional aircraft or astronomical objects. Once a new technology becomes familiar enough to enter public expectations, it can become both the explanation for unidentified sightings and a label mistakenly applied to other things.

That is a major difference from the 1950s. A Blue Book-era witness might have interpreted an unfamiliar aerial light through the technological vocabulary of balloons, aeroplanes, missiles or speculative "flying saucers". A modern observer has another readily available category: the drone. Sometimes that category resolves a UAP; sometimes investigators must determine whether the supposed drone was itself something else.

## Drones changed the baseline, not the meaning of every UAP

The strongest conclusion is therefore narrower than saying that modern UFO reports are "just drones". They are not. AARO's own resolution statistics include balloons, satellites, birds, conventional aircraft, rockets and other explanations, while some reports remain unresolved because the available information is inadequate or because analysis continues.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/UAP-Reporting-Trends/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UAP Trends</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO UAP Trends...</span></span></span>

What drones changed is the **baseline probability landscape**. There are now hundreds of thousands of registered unmanned aircraft in the United States alone, while aviation authorities receive persistent reports of drones near airports. Those machines can occupy low-altitude airspace, hover, manoeuvre in ways unlike fixed-wing aircraft and remain visible chiefly as lights at night.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/exit/?id=gLIKZYBFoMeA4ZCoxTYxAS6EBoFJ%2B2DNJ2yvjzsXYSx3EZFEQKZ9eFvp1Bc7OZHOIIOCZx0MXSvtyl8kA6ywV%2F23YXund%2F5A0RVUQ5jLIrJcERf1%2FFendNjbyjn0IKHYKnfI%2FDf6WshW2njInyDG%2Bi%2FrWM%2BIZc8JkZ%2BrH3jZOsbPhlkNgyg%2Fd3U0g8CG3JNxAoXcc0byfyl0tQUrbPLJ2IgwASeS9sz6B8KzceaU90W1RTo06gc6qICl6a2zHS0%2BTMDTw9y5crTtlAdRcJ6YqUbvc905VVmPgoU%2Bv85N4AGjBVo0txDV9RQlxNaF5%2ByDMK5ZTaCvAmOXh6YJMJ1V0Dj71%2BbVJ4qtlHGbrsSzIUD8MZ2Fwh%2BMMC3lmnl2BGU0odijXh%2BW2bMXMcnwz%2Fe0DgS3gx2GF6HJyFaz6Y%2FoFTl05GmUrgkEja3PtCcvoG5F4ufdE2L6KwWANtFBFvDkjIbgtCmFa%2BTQya7A3oleRzrfXZroUb81CrXl0OMU1XhjddgT7zm%2ByRNEUbkgBZcuFAQ1iOtVxeh275cyLpxOew5SrYbOriKQmc90qjeH8SIA2FYdl1pAYqPCYvao1un68sL3MCDagUUBFTkiJCFbc0qw3Ygt7O1fa7%2BMhgyaKNY70piNvOsBP1p6I2ec2POHDv3Bs1t8k16qlsRTpFV%2F2BbUfRLF0aPNRr5Dh%2FnR8sRvCaZfYqRdymQuv9NWgWTsWg%2FHhRwuQa5ovgIZmu%2BywyPGBWA%2BIGb03I65L3yo1KffoNILfWM0X3lhgtLlMgZwRmb0at0dyF%2BPg4LRRdXY5cPWHC9F3TSFgMPgvzUcCHiMnYbIp1qZ4GR8gv%2Fzt%2BPsHnocI3rcTsbDF4AYgYNQu8dXjLkgxr4zQBKy2YRe8KJuCsu%2FO7b5%2BCi2tp08qtLQ2S8QxkfQCkjIKR4CgbakL72%2FerBCq4IKcLCg1fgTSCEosyoW4hPpBaoDTYkdxfei0nBjo1FCOgbXYOo%2FjA9TA5N2P3zbK%2ByJIRcrKkIEnoDzZsz1%2Fm06BAs1cAeZdKNx2A%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on faa.gov.</span></span></span>

That makes them a historically new and quantitatively significant source of UFO/UAP reports. Project Blue Book investigators could largely leave consumer quadcopters off their list of candidate explanations because the category did not yet exist in practical terms. A modern investigator cannot. Before treating an apparently hovering, rapidly manoeuvring or brightly lit object as evidence of genuinely anomalous flight, today's UAP triage has to eliminate a source that mid-century investigators scarcely had to consider at all: the small unmanned aircraft.
