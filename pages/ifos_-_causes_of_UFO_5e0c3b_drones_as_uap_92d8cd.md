## Introduction

Small drones can reproduce several behaviours that witnesses commonly describe as “UFO-like”: they can hover in one place, move sideways without a conventional turn, stop abruptly, reverse direction and carry lights bright enough to hide the aircraft’s actual outline. At night, a drone may therefore be perceived not as a recognisable machine but simply as a luminous point executing movements that ordinary aeroplanes cannot easily perform. Multirotor drones really can make unusually abrupt manoeuvres; experimental quadcopters have demonstrated high accelerations and complex three-dimensional trajectories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04048" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Accurate Tracking of Aggressive Quadrotor Trajectories using Incremental Nonlinear Dynamic Inversion and Differential FlatnessSeptem...</span></span></span>

## Why drones are difficult to judge at distance

The basic difficulty is that seeing a drone and identifying a drone are two different tasks. A small multirotor that is obvious at close range rapidly becomes a featureless speck with increasing [distance]({{ 'distance/' | relative_url }}). A field study of small-drone visibility found that the probability of detection declined substantially with distance, illustrating how quickly a compact aircraft approaches the limits of useful human visual observation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/10/16/5501" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mdpi.com">[MDPI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mdpi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Distance and Visual Angle of Line-of-Sight of a Small Droneby KW Li · 2020 · Cited by 19 — The purpose of this study is to determine...</span></span></span> Related work cited in human-drone perception research found that, under favourable conditions, small consumer drones could become difficult for many observers to detect at distances of only a few hundred metres.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10416936" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: par.nsf.gov">[National Science Foundation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">par.nsf.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Science FoundationExamining Distance in UAV Gesture Perception*by K Jelonek · 2022 · Cited by 1 — The study found that 245 m was...</span></span></span>

At night the mismatch can be even greater: the airframe disappears before its lights do. US rules for many commercial night operations require small drones to carry anti-collision lighting visible from at least three statute miles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/uas/commercial_operators/operations_over_people" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on faa.gov.</span></span></span> That means a witness may see the light from much farther away than they could ever resolve the drone producing it.

This creates several perceptual traps.

**The apparent object can seem much larger than the real aircraft.** A bright light does not reveal the dimensions of the body carrying it. If a witness assumes that an unresolved light is kilometres away when it is actually hundreds of metres away, any inferred physical size will be greatly exaggerated.

**Distance and speed become entangled.** What the eye directly observes is a change in direction across the [field of view]({{ 'field-of-view/' | relative_url }}). The same angular movement could represent a nearby object moving modestly or a faraway object travelling very quickly. Without an independent range measurement, an apparently astonishing speed may simply be an artefact of the assumed distance.

**[Brightness]({{ 'brightness/' | relative_url }}) can obscure structure.** A small, intense source spreads across neighbouring pixels in a camera and may also produce glare to the naked eye. The recorded object can consequently look like a glowing orb rather than a drone with arms, propellers and a fuselage.

**Sound is not a reliable rescue clue.** Multirotors produce a characteristic high-pitched propeller noise at close range, but that sound becomes harder to distinguish as distance increases or where wind, traffic or other ambient noise is present. Experts consulted by the Associated Press during the 2024 US drone-sighting wave similarly cautioned that a distant nighttime light may provide too little information to distinguish a drone confidently from an aeroplane or another object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/57a0d051a2a94d21787089ceaf47b175" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apnews.com.</span></span></span>

A particularly revealing feature of that episode was the gap between *reports of drones* and *confirmed drones*. In December 2024, the FBI and Department of Homeland Security said investigators were still determining whether reported [New Jersey]({{ 'new-jersey/' | relative_url }}) objects were actually drones, crewed aircraft or inaccurate identifications.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fbi.gov/news/press-releases/joint-dhs-fbi-statement-on-reports-of-drones-in-new-jersey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">joint dhs fbi statement on reports of drones in new jersey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">joint dhs fbi statement on reports of drones in new jersey</span></span></span> A subsequent joint statement from the FBI, DHS, FAA and Department of Defense stressed that large numbers of lawful commercial, recreational and law-enforcement drones were operating in US airspace, adding a genuine drone population to an already complicated visual environment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fbi.gov/news/press-releases/dhs-fbi-faa-and-dod-joint-statement-on-ongoing-response-to-reported-drone-sightings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DHS, FBI, FAA, and Do D Joint Statement on Ongoing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DHS, FBI, FAA, and Do D Joint Statement on Ongoing</span></span></span>

That distinction matters for UFO investigation. Sometimes a drone is mistaken for something exotic. In other cases, an ordinary aircraft or [astronomical]({{ 'astronomical/' | relative_url }}) source is first mistaken for a drone, after which the “drone” label itself acquires mysterious characteristics as reports accumulate.

## Hovering lights and abrupt manoeuvres

Multirotor drones do something fixed-wing aircraft generally cannot: they can generate thrust while maintaining essentially the same position over the ground. Hovering is an ordinary design capability rather than evidence of exotic propulsion. Research on multirotor control treats sustained hover as a fundamental operating mode, including work specifically aimed at improving quadrotor stability while hovering.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mdpi.com/2504-3110/8/11/634" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mdpi.com">[MDPI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mdpi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on mdpi.com.</span></span></span>

The same flight architecture permits sharp changes of direction. A quadcopter alters the speed of individual rotors to change attitude and thrust direction. It does not need a long banking turn of the kind expected from a conventional aeroplane. From a witness's perspective, it can therefore appear to stop, pivot, move laterally or reverse in a manner that feels aerodynamically “wrong” if the observer is mentally comparing it with an aeroplane.

This is not merely a qualitative difference. Researchers developing high-performance quadrotor controllers have demonstrated flight at up to 12.9 metres per second with accelerations reaching about 2.1 g while following complex trajectories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04048" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Accurate Tracking of Aggressive Quadrotor Trajectories using Incremental Nonlinear Dynamic Inversion and Differential FlatnessSeptem...</span></span></span> Such laboratory performance should not be treated as representative of every consumer drone, but it illustrates why “it changed direction too quickly to be an aircraft” is not, by itself, a strong argument against a small unmanned aircraft.

Lights make these manoeuvres harder to interpret. Consider a drone travelling towards an observer at night. With little sideways motion against the background, its bright forward-facing light may appear almost stationary. If it then turns sideways, its angular displacement suddenly becomes obvious. To the witness, a light that had been “hovering” can seem to shoot away without warning even though the drone simply changed its course relative to the line of sight.

Several other ordinary flight patterns can produce striking reports:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Hover, then departure:** station-keeping followed by ordinary forward flight can resemble an instantaneous transition from stationary to rapid motion if the range is badly estimated.
* **Sideways translation:** multirotors can move laterally without first rotating their entire body in the conspicuous way an observer might expect.
* **Rapid yaw:** the drone can rotate about its vertical axis while remaining in roughly the same place. If different lights face different directions, this may look like lights appearing, vanishing or changing colour.
* **Several drones together:** independently controlled aircraft can form lines, triangles or loose clusters. Their spacing may appear geometrically precise when viewed from one angle, especially when the airframes themselves are invisible.
* **A light abruptly “going out”:** a drone need not have disappeared or accelerated away. Its brightest lamp may simply have turned away from the observer, been obscured by the airframe or fallen below the camera's detection threshold.

</div>

Real-world drone incidents show that prolonged nighttime activity and groups of aircraft are entirely feasible without invoking anomalous technology. AARO's FY2025 report recorded 50 unmanned-aircraft incidents supplied by US nuclear-security authorities. Thirty-three involved a single UAS and the remainder involved between two and seven; 47 of the 50 occurred between 17:00 and 05:00, and some lasted an hour or more. Quadcopters were the most frequently reported form. Importantly, AARO explicitly said these particular incidents were reported as UAS incidents rather than UAP.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/FY25%20UAP%20Annual%20Report/AARO_FY2025_Consolidated_Annual_Report_on_UAP.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP...</span></span></span>

That last point is useful precisely because the events were *not* mysterious. Multiple drones, flying at night near sensitive locations for extended periods, are an observed operational reality. Similar behaviour in a setting where observers lack identifying information can readily become the raw material of a UFO report.

## Camera geometry can manufacture extraordinary motion

Drones are especially vulnerable to misinterpretation when the camera itself is moving. The key effect is motion [parallax]({{ 'parallax/' | relative_url }}): relative movement between the observer, background and target can make a slow nearby object appear to cross the scene far faster than it actually travels.

AARO's analysis of the well-known 2013 Aguadilla, [Puerto Rico]({{ 'puerto-rico/' | relative_url }}) infrared recording provides a useful demonstration of the mechanism, even though AARO ultimately assessed the objects as probable sky lanterns rather than drones. The video appeared to show objects moving rapidly around an airport, separating and seemingly interacting with the sea. By reconstructing the aircraft's own flight path and the infrared sensor's viewing geometry, AARO calculated that the objects were actually drifting at roughly wind speed. It attributed their apparent high velocity to motion parallax combined with the aircraft's movement, sensor zoom and changing line of sight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/case_resolution_reports/AARO_Puerto_Rico_UAP_Case_Resolution.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Puerto Rico UAP Case Resolution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Puerto Rico UAP Case Resolution</span></span></span>

The lesson transfers directly to drone reports. A video can show dramatic *image-plane motion* without establishing dramatic physical acceleration. Before concluding that a drone-sized light performed an extraordinary manoeuvre, an investigator needs to know whether the apparent change might instead have resulted from:

**Camera movement.** A small pan or rotation can shift an unresolved target rapidly across the frame.

**Observer movement.** Footage recorded from a car, aircraft or other moving platform adds another velocity vector that must be separated from the target's own motion.

**Zoom changes.** Digital or optical zoom can alter the apparent rate of motion and make small hand movements look much larger.

**Unknown range.** Angular velocity alone does not establish linear velocity. A trustworthy calculation needs a defensible estimate of distance.

**Loss of visual contact.** A light disappearing between frames is not evidence that an object physically vanished. It may have dimmed, rotated, entered cloud, passed behind an obstruction or fallen below the sensor's sensitivity.

AARO's recent reporting reflects how decisive such missing measurements can be. Of 319 reports covered by its FY2025 annual assessment, 191 were transferred to an active archive because corroborating information was insufficient to establish whether their performance was ordinary or genuinely outside known technological limits. AARO explicitly identified the continuing lack of timely, actionable sensor data as a constraint on case resolution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/FY25%20UAP%20Annual%20Report/AARO_FY2025_Consolidated_Annual_Report_on_UAP.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP...</span></span></span>

That is why the most dramatic phrase in a witness report — “instant acceleration”, for example — is better treated as a proposition to be tested than as a measured property of the object.

## Separating drone behaviour from genuinely unknown motion

A drone hypothesis becomes stronger when several independent observations fit together. Hovering by itself is weak evidence; hovering combined with low altitude, multirotor-like directional changes, intermittent nearby buzzing, lighting compatible with a small aircraft and a plausible operating location is much more informative. Conversely, declaring an object a drone simply because it behaves strangely is circular reasoning.

The strongest investigations therefore try to replace impressions with measurable quantities.

**Establish the observation geometry first.** Investigators need the observer's position, viewing direction, elevation angle and, wherever possible, target range. Buildings, ridgelines, clouds and mapped landmarks can sometimes provide useful distance constraints.

**Preserve the original recording.** Cropped, stabilised, recompressed or slowed social-media footage can remove metadata and conceal camera movements. The full sequence before and after the interesting manoeuvre is often more valuable than a dramatic excerpt.

**Compare against local aviation activity.** Conventional aircraft must remain in the candidate set because distant aeroplanes seen almost head-on can also look stationary. During the 2024 US sightings, aviation specialists repeatedly stressed how difficult it was to classify isolated nighttime lights from appearance alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/57a0d051a2a94d21787089ceaf47b175" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apnews.com.</span></span></span>

**Look for actual multirotor signatures rather than generic strangeness.** Sharp lateral translation, station-keeping and abrupt changes between hover and movement are consistent with multirotor operation. They do not prove it, but they are more diagnostic than simply describing something as “fast” or “silent”.

**Demand better evidence before accepting extraordinary performance.** A claim that a light crossed kilometres in a second requires the kilometres to have been measured, not inferred from an assumed object size. Likewise, an apparent right-angle turn is not a physically quantified acceleration unless frame timing, range and trajectory are known.

This evidential discipline cuts both ways. It prevents ordinary drones from being upgraded into apparently impossible craft, but it also prevents “drone” from becoming a catch-all explanation applied to every unresolved report. AARO's FY2025 findings illustrate the distinction: it resolved hundreds of cases as ordinary objects, including UAS, yet still retained reports for further analysis or archival review when the necessary data were absent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/FY25%20UAP%20Annual%20Report/AARO_FY2025_Consolidated_Annual_Report_on_UAP.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP...</span></span></span>

## The practical takeaway

Drones deserve particular attention in modern UFO/UAP investigations because their real capabilities overlap unusually well with behaviours that can seem anomalous to a distant observer. Hovering, lateral movement, abrupt stops, reversals, formation flight and conspicuous lighting are all technologically ordinary for the relevant classes of unmanned aircraft. Their small physical size also means the body can disappear from view while a bright light remains visible, stripping away the visual clues normally used to estimate distance and speed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/uas/commercial_operators/operations_over_people" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on faa.gov.</span></span></span>

But the useful conclusion is not that strange aerial behaviour is “probably a drone” in every case. It is that drone-like explanations should be tested quantitatively before unusual motion is treated as genuinely anomalous. Recent AARO reporting demonstrates both sides of that standard: unmanned aircraft continue to appear among resolved UAP cases, while cases lacking sufficient sensor data are not automatically assigned an ordinary explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/FY25%20UAP%20Annual%20Report/AARO_FY2025_Consolidated_Annual_Report_on_UAP.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP...</span></span></span>

For a distant nighttime sighting, the decisive question is therefore rarely whether the light *looked* extraordinary. It is whether the observation contains enough independent information to establish the object's range, trajectory and acceleration. Until those quantities are constrained, a small hovering drone carrying a bright light can look far more mysterious than its actual flight performance warrants.
