## Introduction

Military illumination flares can produce one of the more convincing kinds of UFO or UAP light display: several brilliant points apparently hovering in formation, drifting sideways, descending almost imperceptibly and then vanishing one after another. The effect is not accidental. Large illumination flares are designed to remain aloft under parachutes for minutes while casting intense light over terrain. A US Air Force manual, for example, describes the LUU-2 aircraft parachute flare as producing roughly two million candlepower for about five minutes while descending thousands of feet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static.e-publishing.af.mil/production/1/af_a3/publication/afman11-2mc-130jv3/afman11-2mc-130jv3.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static.e-publishing.af.mil">[E-Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static.e-publishing.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on af.mil.</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-overview"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/ifos_-_causes_of_UFO_5e0c3b_flares_ufo_reports_714a4a-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Why Military Flares" width="1536" height="1024" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Illustrative overview</span></figcaption></figure>

That combination of long duration, slow descent and extreme [brightness]({{ 'brightness/' | relative_url }}) removes many of the visual cues people normally use to judge [distance]({{ 'distance/' | relative_url }}), scale and speed at night. Wind can carry separate flares together, while staggered release or burnout can make a row of lights seem to appear or extinguish deliberately. The strongest flare explanation, however, is not simply that a sighting “looks like flares”. It is one in which the sighting's time, direction, behaviour and duration also match documented military activity.

## How parachute flares can seem to hover

Illumination flares are fundamentally different from the brief defensive flares sometimes seen pouring from military aircraft. A US Federal Register notice describing training ordnance distinguishes short-lived self-protection flares, which burn for less than ten seconds, from LUU-2 high-intensity illumination flares. The latter are ejected from an aircraft, deploy a parachute after a timed delay and burn for approximately five minutes while suspended beneath it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-06-08/pdf/2010-13748.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>

The Air Force's MC-130J manual gives an unusually useful description of what that means in the sky. It specifies an LUU-2/B or 2A/B illumination time of approximately 300 seconds and recommends a release geometry in which the burning flare descends about 2,500 feet before burnout.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static.e-publishing.af.mil/production/1/af_a3/publication/afman11-2mc-130jv3/afman11-2mc-130jv3.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static.e-publishing.af.mil">[E-Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static.e-publishing.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on af.mil.</span></span></span> That is slow enough that, at a distance of many kilometres, the downward movement may occupy only a tiny angle in an observer's [field of view]({{ 'field-of-view/' | relative_url }}). Without a visible foreground reference, a steadily descending source can therefore look nearly stationary.

The parachute also makes the path less like a falling projectile and more like a drifting aerial light. Air Force safety guidance explicitly treats the flare's **rate of descent and drift** as variables when determining safe release altitude and distance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static.e-publishing.af.mil/production/1/af_a3/publication/afman11-2mc-130jv3/afman11-2mc-130jv3.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static.e-publishing.af.mil">[E-Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static.e-publishing.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on af.mil.</span></span></span> A flare can consequently move laterally with winds while sinking slowly, especially when different winds act at different heights.

Its brightness compounds the problem. Federal documentation rates the LUU-2B at roughly 1.8 million candlepower and says that from 1,000 feet it can illuminate a circle about 500 metres across. It burns magnesium at high temperature, producing an intense white light.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-06-08/pdf/2010-13748.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span> From far away at night, the observer is therefore much more likely to see an isolated point of light than the parachute, smoke, flare body or aircraft responsible for it.

This is an important identification trap. A witness may accurately report that a light was bright, silent and apparently hovering; none of those observations by itself establishes that the source was physically hovering. Distance and altitude are generally unknown, and sound from an aircraft or flare release may never reach the observer at a useful level.

## Why several flares can look like a formation

A group of parachute flares can be substantially stranger than a single one because the eye naturally connects nearby lights into a larger pattern. Several releases can form an arc, line or loose geometric arrangement even though the lights are independent objects.

Three features are especially characteristic.

**Slow common drift.** Flares released into broadly similar winds can move in approximately the same direction. Their separation may change gradually as individual parachutes encounter slightly different airflow. From a distant viewpoint, that resembles a formation changing shape rather than unrelated objects being carried by wind. Air Force operating guidance specifically identifies drift as part of flare behaviour rather than treating the devices as vertically falling lights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static.e-publishing.af.mil/production/1/af_a3/publication/afman11-2mc-130jv3/afman11-2mc-130jv3.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static.e-publishing.af.mil">[E-Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static.e-publishing.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on af.mil.</span></span></span>

**Long, overlapping burn periods.** Five-minute illumination times allow several independently released flares to coexist in the sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static.e-publishing.af.mil/production/1/af_a3/publication/afman11-2mc-130jv3/afman11-2mc-130jv3.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static.e-publishing.af.mil">[E-Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static.e-publishing.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on af.mil.</span></span></span> If an aircraft releases them successively, the resulting lights need not ignite or extinguish together. A viewer may instead see one light appear, followed by another, with older flares going out while newer ones remain visible. That can suggest coordinated switching, a rotating formation or lights mounted on an unseen craft.

**Abrupt disappearance.** A flare does not have to fade gradually from view. It can burn out, be hidden by terrain or disappear into cloud or haze. The LUU-2 mechanism itself also changes at the end of its operating cycle: federal documentation says that after candle burnout an explosive bolt releases a parachute support cable so that the parachute collapses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-06-08/pdf/2010-13748.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span> From a distant viewpoint, the luminous point can consequently disappear with little visual warning.

There is another subtle effect. The same Federal Register description says that as the LUU-2 candle consumes its housing, the flare becomes lighter and its rate of fall slows during roughly the final two minutes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-06-08/pdf/2010-13748.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span> The intuitive expectation that a falling object should continually accelerate is therefore particularly misleading here: the parachute system is deliberately controlling the descent.

## Phoenix shows both the strength and the danger of the flare explanation

The 13 March 1997 “Phoenix Lights” episode is the best-known illustration of why flare identification requires careful separation of events. Reports accumulated across Arizona over several hours, and accounts that are often grouped under one name did not all describe the same phenomenon. Later reporting and investigation distinguished an earlier travelling formation from a second display of brilliant lights south-west of Phoenix at around 10 p.m.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/2015/03/alien-lights-at-phoenix-stephenville-and-elsewhere-a-postmortem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Skeptical Inquirer Alien Lights?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">At Phoenix, Stephenville, and ElsewhereMarch 1, 2015 — At around 8:30 on the night of March 13, 1997, residents “flares do not fly in for...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1, 2015</span></span></span>

The case nevertheless demonstrates a major investigative risk: **an explanation for one time window should not automatically be applied to every report made that night**. The earlier Phoenix observations involved moving V- or chevron-shaped groups of lights and occurred well before the documented late-evening flare display. ## Wind, terrain and viewing geometry can create misleading motion

Night-time light sightings are particularly vulnerable to errors in apparent motion because the observer normally lacks a known distance. A light descending vertically a long way away may appear almost fixed against the sky, while a moderate crosswind can produce more noticeable sideways movement than downward movement. The result can be described quite reasonably as “hovering and then moving sideways”.

Terrain can make the sequence look more deliberate still. In the Phoenix case, the Sierra Estrella range provided a foreground horizon that was difficult to perceive in darkness. A descending light crossing behind such a ridge can seem simply to switch off. Multiple flares at slightly different heights will cross the same hidden horizon at different times, creating a procession of apparent extinctions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.phoenixnewtimes.com/arts-culture/the-phoenix-lights-are-no-mystery-6661825/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: phoenixnewtimes.com">[Phoenix New Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">phoenixnewtimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Phoenix New TimesThe 1997 &quot;Phoenix Lights&quot; are no mysteryMarch 14, 2014 — 14 Mar 2014 — Channel 12 filmed a similar dropping of flares by...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 14, 2014</span></span></span>

Brightness can also conceal useful references. A powerful point source can dominate a camera exposure and erase faint stars or landscape detail that would otherwise reveal its motion. This is one reason video showing a row of saturated blobs against a black sky often contains less positional information than its dramatic appearance suggests. Researchers examining the Phoenix material have argued that intense lights and poor background reference contributed to impressions of a coherent structure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/2015/03/alien-lights-at-phoenix-stephenville-and-elsewhere-a-postmortem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Skeptical Inquirer Alien Lights?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">At Phoenix, Stephenville, and ElsewhereMarch 1, 2015 — At around 8:30 on the night of March 13, 1997, residents “flares do not fly in for...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1, 2015</span></span></span>

The reverse caution is equally important: movement inconsistent with plausible wind-driven descent should count against a flare hypothesis. A clearly measured object accelerating horizontally, reversing repeatedly against the local wind, maintaining a fixed low altitude for far longer than the relevant flare's burn time, or travelling through a well-constrained three-dimensional path would require additional explanation. “Flares can drift” should not become a catch-all excuse for behaviour that has not actually been modelled.

## Matching a sighting to military activity

Visual resemblance is best treated as the start of a flare investigation, not its conclusion. The most persuasive identifications combine several independent matches.

A useful assessment asks whether:

* **The time matches.** Training, weapons-range or illumination activity should overlap the sighting to within a plausible interval. A flare exercise two hours later cannot explain an earlier observation merely because it occurred on the same night.
* **The bearing matches.** The lights should appear in the direction of the training range or release area when viewed from the witness location. Multiple geographically separated videos can be especially valuable because intersecting lines of sight constrain the source region.
* **The duration fits the flare type.** Long-lived parachute illumination flares can remain visible for several minutes; short defensive countermeasure flares normally cannot. Federal documentation explicitly distinguishes LUU-2 illumination flares with approximately five-minute burns from countermeasure flares lasting under ten seconds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-06-08/pdf/2010-13748.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>
* **The vertical behaviour fits.** Slow descent, eventual disappearance near a horizon and changing apparent spacing are expected. Air Force documentation confirms that illumination-flare planning must account for burn time, descent rate and drift.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static.e-publishing.af.mil/production/1/af_a3/publication/afman11-2mc-130jv3/afman11-2mc-130jv3.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static.e-publishing.af.mil">[E-Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static.e-publishing.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on af.mil.</span></span></span>
* **Wind is compatible.** Winds at the flare's altitude matter more than surface wind. A convincing [reconstruction]({{ 'reconstruction/' | relative_url }}) should not assume that a ground-level breeze represents conditions thousands of feet above.
* **Terrain is checked.** Digital elevation data or a daylight photograph from the same location can reveal whether an apparently mid-air extinction actually coincides with a distant ridge.
* **Operational records support the claim.** Unit histories, range schedules, official statements, flight logs or credible contemporary reporting are much stronger evidence than the mere presence of a military base somewhere in the region.

The evidential hierarchy matters. “There is a bombing range nearby” establishes possibility. “Aircraft on that range were documented releasing the relevant flare type during the precise sighting window, in the observed direction, with trajectories matching the video” approaches an identification.

Conversely, failure to find a public exercise notice does not prove that the lights were extraordinary. Detailed military training records may be incomplete, unavailable or published long after an event. The appropriate result in that situation may simply be **consistent with flares but unconfirmed**.

## When the flare explanation is strong — and when it is not

Parachute illumination flares deserve serious consideration when a UFO report describes extremely bright lights that remain visible for minutes, seem nearly stationary or drift gently, occur in a line or arc, and disappear sequentially over or near a military training area. Those features arise naturally from equipment expressly designed to produce powerful, slow-descending airborne illumination. US Air Force documentation confirms five-minute burn times, parachute suspension, substantial descent during operation and operational concern with wind drift.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static.e-publishing.af.mil/production/1/af_a3/publication/afman11-2mc-130jv3/afman11-2mc-130jv3.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static.e-publishing.af.mil">[E-Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static.e-publishing.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on af.mil.</span></span></span>

They are especially persuasive where an independent activity record closes the loop. The late-evening component of the Phoenix Lights is valuable precisely because the flare hypothesis does not depend only on resemblance: military training activity was reported for the relevant night, the lights appeared towards the range, and later flare exercises reproduced important features of the recorded display.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.deseret.com/1997/7/26/19325702/flares-not-ufos-caused-light-show-military-says/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: deseret.com">[Deseret News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">deseret.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">flares not ufos caused light show military says</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deseret NewsFlares, not UFOs, caused light show, military says26 Jul 1997 — Visiting jets from the Maryland Air National Guard were using...</span></span></span>

But flares should not be used as a generic label for every unexplained orange or white light. Investigators should be suspicious of an explanation that requires changing the sighting time, ignoring its direction, confusing short countermeasure flares with long-burning illumination devices or merging distinct episodes because they occurred in the same region. Phoenix itself demonstrates why that discipline matters: the strongest flare evidence pertains to a particular late-evening light display, not indiscriminately to every observation reported across Arizona that night.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/2015/03/alien-lights-at-phoenix-stephenville-and-elsewhere-a-postmortem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Skeptical Inquirer Alien Lights?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">At Phoenix, Stephenville, and ElsewhereMarch 1, 2015 — At around 8:30 on the night of March 13, 1997, residents “flares do not fly in for...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1, 2015</span></span></span>

The practical lesson for UFO and UAP identification is therefore narrower but more useful than “strange lights are often flares”. Parachute flares have a distinctive physical signature: **very high brightness, minute-scale persistence, controlled descent, wind-driven drift and potentially staggered extinction**. When those characteristics align with the event's exact time window and documented military activity, an apparently hovering formation can be explained without requiring anything in the sky to have hovered at all.
