## Introduction

A distant ship can appear to be an airborne object when atmospheric refraction alters its position or shape near the horizon. The best-known modern example is the striking set of photographs taken near Falmouth, Cornwall, in early 2021, showing a tanker apparently suspended above the sea. The images were widely identified as a **superior mirage**, produced when cold air near the water lay beneath warmer air and bent light from the distant vessel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2021/mar/05/ship-hovering-above-sea-cornwall-optical-illusion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianWalker &#x27;stunned&#x27; to see ship hovering high above sea off Cornwall &#124; Science &#124; The GuardianMarch 5, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 5, 2021</span></span></span>

The case is useful for understanding some UFO and UAP reports because the source is not mysterious: there really is a ship in the [distance]({{ 'distance/' | relative_url }}). What becomes misleading is its apparent relationship to the horizon. Genuine superior [mirages]({{ 'mirages/' | relative_url }}) can raise, stretch, invert and duplicate ships, sometimes making them look remarkably unlike ordinary vessels. But the Cornwall photographs also carry an important caution: their precise optical explanation has been disputed. They therefore illustrate both the power of horizon effects and the danger of assigning a specific mirage mechanism from appearance alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/mirage.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cloudatlas.wmo.int">[International Cloud Atlas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cloudatlas.wmo.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Cloud Atlas Mirage &#124; International Cloud Atlas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Cloud Atlas Mirage &#124; International Cloud Atlas</span></span></span>

## What happened in the Cornwall photographs?

In March 2021, photographs by David Morris attracted international attention after he saw a large tanker apparently hovering above the sea while looking out from Gillan, near Falmouth. The vessel itself remained recognisable, but a conspicuous band apparently separated its hull from the visible water. Morris described being stunned by the sight. The Guardian, Independent and CBS News subsequently reported the phenomenon as a superior mirage, citing meteorologist David Braine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2021/mar/05/ship-hovering-above-sea-cornwall-optical-illusion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[theguardian.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianWalker &#x27;stunned&#x27; to see ship hovering high above sea off Cornwall &#124; Science &#124; The GuardianMarch 5, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 5, 2021</span></span></span>

The interpretation was physically plausible in general terms. A temperature inversion reverses the [atmosphere]({{ 'atmosphere/' | relative_url }})'s usual pattern: instead of temperature simply falling with height, a layer of warmer air sits above cooler air. The Met Office describes such inversions as layers in which temperature increases with height, with cooler air trapped underneath warmer air. Over a cold sea, that arrangement can provide exactly the refractive environment associated with superior mirages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/learn-about/weather/types-of-weather/temperature/temperature-inversion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: weather.metoffice.gov.uk">[Met Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">weather.metoffice.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Met Office What is a temperature inversion?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Met OfficeWhat is a temperature inversion? - Met Office...</span></span></span>

Braine's explanation was that cold air lay close to the relatively cold sea with warmer air above. Because the colder air was denser and had a slightly higher refractive index, light from the distant ship could curve downwards on its route towards an observer on the coast. The observer, effectively extrapolating those arriving rays backwards in straight lines, would perceive the source higher than its true position.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2021/mar/05/ship-hovering-above-sea-cornwall-optical-illusion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianWalker &#x27;stunned&#x27; to see ship hovering high above sea off Cornwall &#124; Science &#124; The GuardianMarch 5, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 5, 2021</span></span></span>

The timing made the photographs especially memorable. A second set of apparently hovering vessels was photographed off Dorset less than two weeks later, including the cruise ship *Jewel of the Seas*. The Guardian again attributed the appearance to cold air over the sea beneath warmer air. With numerous cruise ships anchored off southern England during the pandemic, large and readily identifiable targets happened to be available near the horizon when favourable optical conditions occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2021/mar/18/pop-up-enabled-ship-hover-dorset-coast-superior-mirage" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianSecond vessel in two weeks appears to float above UK waters &#124; Science &#124; The GuardianMarch 18, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 18, 2021</span></span></span>

That combination matters. These were not vague points of light whose identity had to be guessed. They were large conventional vessels that looked, at first glance, as though they occupied physically impossible positions. The photographs therefore offer an unusually intuitive demonstration of how an observation can be authentic while the observer's natural interpretation of its geometry is wrong.

## How can an inversion lift the apparent image?

A superior mirage does not literally pick up an object's image and move it like a photograph being dragged upwards. Light from different parts of a distant object travels through slightly different portions of an atmosphere whose density and refractive index change with height. The resulting ray paths can curve by different amounts. The World Meteorological Organization's International Cloud Atlas notes that mirages can consequently produce single or multiple images that are upright or inverted and vertically enlarged or reduced; objects may also appear at substantially different angles above the horizon from their true positions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/mirage.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cloudatlas.wmo.int">[International Cloud Atlas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cloudatlas.wmo.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Cloud Atlas Mirage &#124; International Cloud Atlas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Cloud Atlas Mirage &#124; International Cloud Atlas</span></span></span>

Ships provide particularly clear demonstrations because they have recognisable vertical structures — hulls, masts, funnels and superstructures — against a comparatively simple sea-and-sky background. Atmospheric Optics documents superior ship mirages in which an inverted vessel appears above the ordinary image and, under more complicated conditions, another upright image appears higher still. The exact result depends on the vertical temperature profile rather than merely on the existence of an inversion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atoptics.org.uk/fz782.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atoptics.org.uk">[Atmospheric Optics]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atoptics.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atmospheric Optics OPOD</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atmospheric OpticsOPOD - 4 ship superior mirageMarch 25, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 25, 2012</span></span></span>

This distinction separates several effects that can look superficially similar. **Looming** primarily changes apparent position, allowing a distant object to appear higher than normal or even bringing something geometrically below the horizon into sight. Stronger or more complicated differential refraction can stretch, squash, invert or multiply parts of an image. A particularly elaborate superior mirage can produce the shifting, stacked distortions commonly associated with a [Fata Morgana]({{ 'fata-morgana/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/mirage.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cloudatlas.wmo.int">[International Cloud Atlas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cloudatlas.wmo.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Cloud Atlas Mirage &#124; International Cloud Atlas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Cloud Atlas Mirage &#124; International Cloud Atlas</span></span></span>

Historical marine observations show how dramatic this can become. The technical chapter on optical mirages in the Condon UFO study reproduced maritime observations in which ships underwent marked vertical extension and distortion. In another 1955 observation near Cape Town, a tanker about eight miles away appeared so distorted that its funnel became greatly elongated and seemed taller than its masts. Such examples are valuable because the target's underlying identity was known: atmospheric propagation had transformed an ordinary ship into an unfamiliar-looking object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s6chap04.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 4: Optical Mirage</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCondon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 4: Optical Mirage...</span></span></span>

There is also an important geometrical constraint. The Condon study emphasised that a steep temperature gradient by itself is not sufficient. Light must encounter the strongly refracting layer at a shallow, grazing angle. Superior mirages are consequently most relevant to objects lying close to the apparent horizon, rather than objects genuinely high overhead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s6chap04.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 4: Optical Mirage</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCondon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 4: Optical Mirage...</span></span></span>

## Was the Cornwall ship definitely a superior mirage?

The widely reported answer was yes, but the photographs deserve more careful treatment than their viral captions usually received.

The initial reporting was not completely uniform. ITV's West Country report on 4 March 2021 described a simpler visual explanation involving the similar colouring of distant sea and sky, which could make the water around the ship difficult to distinguish and create the appearance of empty sky beneath it. Subsequent national and international reports instead presented Braine's superior-mirage explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.itv.com/news/westcountry/2021-03-04/man-left-shocked-as-large-ship-appears-to-float-in-the-sky" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: itv.com">[ITVX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">itv.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">XMan left shocked as large ship appears to float in the sky &#124; ITV News West CountryMarch 4, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 4, 2021</span></span></span>

Independent discussion of the photographs has also questioned whether their particular appearance is what a superior mirage should produce. A Physics Stack Exchange analysis, for example, pointed out the conspicuous difficulty: the ship looks comparatively undistorted, while the apparent gap underneath it seems to be sky or sky-coloured sea. One proposed alternative is that the misleading feature is therefore the background beneath the vessel rather than a dramatically displaced image of the vessel itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://physics.stackexchange.com/q/619022/123208" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: physics.stackexchange.com">[Physics Stack Exchange]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">physics.stackexchange.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Physics Stack Exchangeoptics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Physics Stack Exchangeoptics</span></span></span>

That criticism should not be confused with a claim that superior ship mirages are doubtful. They are well documented. Atmospheric Optics photographs show ships accompanied by clearly inverted and repeated images, while the World Meteorological Organization explicitly recognises superior mirages over cold sea surfaces. The issue is narrower: **whether this particular famous Cornwall photograph demonstrates precisely the mechanism that popular reports assigned to it**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atoptics.org.uk/fz782.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atoptics.org.uk">[Atmospheric Optics]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atoptics.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atmospheric Optics OPOD</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atmospheric OpticsOPOD - 4 ship superior mirageMarch 25, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 25, 2012</span></span></span>

This makes the Cornwall case more instructive, not less. A photograph can establish that a ship looked astonishingly airborne without, by itself, establishing which atmospheric or contrast effect produced the apparent separation from the water. For UFO analysis, that distinction is fundamental. Identifying an ordinary source is one question; reconstructing the exact optical path that made it look extraordinary is another.

## Why does the same geometry matter for UFO reports?


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

A ship distorted near the horizon is a useful analogue for a particular class of UFO or UAP observation because an observer normally judges an object's altitude partly from its relationship to the horizon and surrounding landscape. Once refraction corrupts those reference points, a surface object can acquire the visual characteristics of something airborne.

</div>

Three changes are especially important. First, **apparent elevation** can detach an object from the surface with which it would normally be associated. Second, **distortion** can remove familiar proportions: a funnel, mast or hull may stretch or compress until the silhouette no longer immediately reads as a ship. Third, **multiple or inverted images** can produce apparent structures that do not correspond to any single physical object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atoptics.org.uk/fz782.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atoptics.org.uk">[Atmospheric Optics]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atoptics.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atmospheric Optics OPOD</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atmospheric OpticsOPOD - 4 ship superior mirageMarch 25, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 25, 2012</span></span></span>

The Condon study's UFO analysis contains historical cases where precisely this possibility was considered. Its optical and [radar]({{ 'radar/' | relative_url }}) chapter discusses reports in which superior mirages of ships beyond the normal horizon were regarded as likely components of unusual-object sightings. Elsewhere, investigators considered atmospheric miraging because reported objects remained close to the observer's horizontal plane and changed shape in ways consistent with strong optical distortion. The report nevertheless stressed that a proposed mirage had to satisfy both the viewing geometry and the meteorological conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s3chap05.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Sec III, Chapter 5: Optical &amp; Radar Analysis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Files Condon Report, Sec III, Chapter 5: Optical &amp; Radar Analysis</span></span></span>

That gives the floating-ship example a more useful lesson than simply saying that "UFOs can be mirages". A credible mirage hypothesis makes testable predictions. The object should generally be close to the horizon or refracting layer; a suitable temperature structure should be possible; a distant source should exist in approximately the required direction; and the observed displacement or distortion should be compatible with atmospheric refraction. Merely finding an inversion somewhere in the weather record is not enough.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s6chap04.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 4: Optical Mirage</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCondon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 4: Optical Mirage...</span></span></span>

Conversely, an apparently airborne object that is well above the horizon, seen through ordinary atmospheric conditions and tracked from several geometrically useful viewpoints would be a much poorer candidate for this explanation. Superior mirages are powerful precisely because their effects can be dramatic within a restricted optical geometry, not because they can arbitrarily relocate anything anywhere in the sky.

## The strongest lesson from a “floating” ship

The most important feature of these photographs is that the observer need not hallucinate, exaggerate or fabricate anything. A camera can record the same extraordinary-looking scene. Atmospheric refraction and horizon contrast are physical effects acting on the light before it reaches either an eye or a sensor.

Well-documented superior mirages provide the strongest version of the demonstration. Ships have been photographed with inverted copies above them, with additional upright images, and with funnels and hulls stretched into shapes that scarcely resemble their ordinary proportions. The International Cloud Atlas confirms that distant objects in mirages can be displaced, multiplied, inverted and vertically distorted, while specialised ship observations show those effects occurring in practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/mirage.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cloudatlas.wmo.int">[wmo.int]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cloudatlas.wmo.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Cloud Atlas Mirage &#124; International Cloud Atlas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Cloud Atlas Mirage &#124; International Cloud Atlas</span></span></span>

The Cornwall photographs add a useful evidential warning. Their airborne appearance is obvious, but the exact mechanism behind that appearance has attracted competing interpretations. For an investigation of an unidentified aerial observation, that is precisely the right distinction to preserve: demonstrating that known atmospheric optics can create convincing “objects in the sky” does not justify labelling every puzzling horizon photograph a superior mirage.

Instead, floating ships establish a narrower and stronger point. **A familiar object at sea level can genuinely be photographed in a configuration that makes it appear airborne, and strong atmospheric refraction can transform distant ships even more radically.** When a UFO or UAP report originates close to a sea horizon, particularly under inversion conditions, investigators therefore have good reason to test for ships and other surface sources before inferring that the reported object's apparent altitude reflects its true physical position.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s6chap04.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 4: Optical Mirage</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCondon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 4: Optical Mirage...</span></span></span>
